Feature Transformation for Numeric Variables

Notebook 2 repaired data that was wrong. Nothing here fixes an error: every value in this notebook is already correct. The problem is that models carry hidden assumptions about scale and shape, and raw numbers rarely satisfy them. This article measures what happens when they don't, including one change of order that turns a model into the single worst result in the entire series before turning it into the best.

Datasets: Telco Churn & California Housing (20,640 rows) Stack: pandas · scikit-learn · matplotlib Companion to: Notebook 3

The first two notebooks in this series both fixed things that were broken: a customer ID that should never have been a feature, a blank cell hiding as a valid string, an age nobody recorded. This one is different in a way that's worth sitting with before the first line of code. Every number in the columns below is accurate. Nobody mistyped anything, nothing is missing, no outlier needs a decision about whether it's real. And several of these correct, error-free numbers are about to make a model perform worse than it would by ignoring the data entirely.

The reason is that a model is never just looking at your numbers. It's looking at them through a specific set of assumptions, and those assumptions are rarely stated out loud. A k-nearest-neighbours classifier assumes that Euclidean distance means something comparable in every column. A linear model assumes that a straight line is a reasonable way to describe a relationship. Squaring a column assumes that column doesn't already contain an extreme value, because squaring one only makes it more extreme. None of these assumptions are announced anywhere, and none of them will raise an error when violated. The model just quietly gets worse, and everything still runs.

This notebook is about closing that gap between what your numbers look like and what your model needs them to look like, across four kinds of transformation: scaling, so that no column dominates a calculation purely by being measured in bigger units; non-linear transforms, so that a skewed column stops being ninety-seven times as lopsided as its neighbor; binning, which turns a smooth number into a set of ranges, for the specific cases where that trade is worth making; and polynomial features, which let a model bend where the data itself curves.

Telco returns from the first two notebooks for the classification work. Alongside it, this notebook brings in California Housing, a 20,640-row census dataset that ships directly with scikit-learn, to supply a regression target and, more importantly, columns that are genuinely and dramatically misshapen in ways Telco's rarely are.

1Why scaling matters

Not an abstract sensitivity to scale. A specific, measurable way that real features go completely unused

The standard explanation for why scaling matters is that "some algorithms are sensitive to the scale of their inputs." That sentence is true and does almost nothing for you, because it gives no way to predict when the problem will bite or how large it will be. Telco's own numeric columns make the failure concrete enough to measure.

Distance-based algorithms, k-nearest neighbours foremost among them, decide which rows count as similar by measuring how far apart they are. Euclidean distance takes the difference in each column, squares it, and adds the squares together. Squaring is the detail that matters: it means a column with a wide numeric range contributes a large number to that sum, and a column with a narrow range contributes a small one, regardless of which column actually carries more useful information about the thing you're trying to predict.

The precise claim, stated once so it's easy to hold onto

Scaling did not add information. The four flags were exactly as predictive before scaling as after; the model simply couldn't use what was already there. That's also what makes this failure dangerous in practice: nothing throws an error, nothing prints a warning, and a genuinely well-engineered feature just sits in the dataset having no effect, for reasons that never show up anywhere in the code.

2The three scalers, and what each does to an outlier

All three put columns on a comparable footing. They disagree, sharply, about what "comparable" should mean

Three formulas, each defining fairness between columns differently:

StandardScaler subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, giving every column a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

z = (x − μ) / σ

MinMaxScaler squeezes a column into a fixed range, 0 to 1 by default.

x' = (x − xmin) / (xmax − xmin)

RobustScaler subtracts the median and divides by the interquartile range.

x' = (x − median) / (Q3 − Q1)

The three formulas already predict how each will behave around an extreme value, if you know to look for it. Notebook 2 spent real effort establishing that the mean and the two most extreme values in a column are not robust statistics: a single huge value can drag them a long way from where most of the data actually sits. StandardScaler is built entirely from the mean and standard deviation. MinMaxScaler is built entirely from the two most extreme values, which makes it the most fragile of the three by construction. RobustScaler is built from the median and the quartiles, which barely move no matter how extreme a handful of outliers get.

California Housing's AveOccup column, average household occupancy per census block, is close to an ideal stress test: a median of 2.82 people, entirely ordinary, against a maximum of 1,243.33, a single block group that is almost certainly a group living facility rather than a household in the usual sense.

The IQR_width column is the one to focus on, since it measures how much room the bulk of ordinary neighborhoods get to spread out in after scaling.

MinMaxScaler fails outright here. The middle 99% of all neighborhoods gets compressed into the range 0.001 to 0.004. Two block groups with genuinely different occupancy rates, one at 0.0021 and another at 0.0023, are now separated by an amount most models will treat as indistinguishable from measurement noise, because one distant outlier is entitled to define the entire top of the scale.

StandardScaler survives, but the outlier still leaves a mark. The middle 99% spans roughly 0.45 of a unit, tighter than it should be, because the single extreme value inflates the standard deviation that every other value gets divided by.

RobustScaler is unmoved. Its interquartile width is exactly 1.0 by construction, and the middle 99% spans about 5.5 units, a sensible amount of room. The outlier still lands far out at 1,455, but it no longer drags everyone else toward the bottom of the scale on its way there.

Choosing between the three

StandardScaler is the sensible default; reach for it unless you have a specific reason not to. RobustScaler when the column carries outliers you've deliberately decided to keep, which is common in financial and behavioral data. MinMaxScaler only when you genuinely need a bounded range, such as pixel values or a network layer that expects inputs between 0 and 1, and only after checking the column for outliers first, since it's the one most easily broken by them.

One property of scaling is easy to assume and worth checking directly rather than taking on faith: does any of this touch the actual shape of the distribution, or only its units?

Every panel reports a skew of 1.647, the exact same figure as the untouched original. Scaling is a linear operation: it slides a distribution along its axis and stretches it uniformly, and neither of those moves changes its shape. A long right tail survives all three scalers completely intact.

This is the single most useful fact to take from this section, precisely because it's also the most commonly confused: scaling changes units, not shape. A skewed column going into a scaler comes out exactly as skewed. Repairing shape is a different job, covered in section 5.

3Which algorithms actually need scaling

Scaling gets applied to every numeric column, reflexively, in a lot of production code. Knowing where it does nothing tells you what it's really for

Trees are the clean case to reason through first. A decision tree splits on questions of the form "is TotalCharges above 1,400?" Multiply every value in that column by 1,000, or subtract its mean, or squeeze it into the unit interval, and the threshold that answers the question changes to a different number, but the actual rows that fall on each side of the split do not. A monotonic rescaling can only ever relabel a threshold. It cannot change which rows are above it and which are below.

Testing that directly: a random forest, raw and scaled
forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=120, random_state=RANDOM_STATE, n_jobs=1)

forest_raw = churn_auc(forest)
forest_scaled = churn_auc(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), forest))

print(f'RandomForest on raw features : AUC {forest_raw:.4f}')
print(f'RandomForest + StandardScaler: AUC {forest_scaled:.4f}')
print(f'Difference                   : {abs(forest_scaled - forest_raw):.4f}')
Output
RandomForest on raw features : AUC 0.8012
RandomForest + StandardScaler: AUC 0.8020
Difference                   : 0.0008

A difference of 0.0008, which is noise rather than a real effect. In exact arithmetic the two numbers would be identical, since a tree only ever compares a value to a threshold and scaling preserves that ordering perfectly. The tiny gap that does show up comes from floating-point rounding: rescaled values land on slightly different candidate split points, and a tie between two similarly good splits can break a different way. Run the same comparison with a different random seed and the sign of that gap can flip.

The practical reading: scaling a tree-based model buys you nothing. It also costs nothing, which is exactly why it's reasonable to put a scaler in a pipeline by default even when today's model happens to be a tree, since tomorrow's model might not be.

The honest result here is that AUC doesn't move at all: 0.8087 either way, on these three columns. That's worth reporting exactly as it happened rather than dressing it up, and it still doesn't mean scaling was pointless, because two other things changed that a single accuracy number would never show you.

Convergence went from 54 iterations down to 11. The optimizer underneath logistic regression searches a space shaped by the columns it's given, and when one column's scale dwarfs the others, that space becomes a long, narrow valley the search has to zigzag along. Scaling makes the valley round instead of elongated. On three columns and 7,000 rows nobody notices a few extra iterations. On a wide dataset with a slower solver, the same effect is the difference between a model that fits and one that never converges at all.

The coefficients became comparable to each other. In the unscaled model, TotalCharges carries a coefficient of 0.00015 and tenure carries −0.06711. Reading the first as unimportant because it's a smaller number is a natural mistake, and it's simply wrong: the two coefficients are expressed in different units; one is "per dollar of total charges," the other is "per month of tenure," and numbers in different units are not comparable by looking at them. After scaling, the same three coefficients become −1.616, 0.913, and 0.299, all in a shared unit of one standard deviation, and now their relative sizes actually mean something you can compare.

A third reason exists that doesn't happen to show up in this particular comparison, and it's worth knowing about before it costs you a model. Ridge and Lasso regression penalize the size of their coefficients directly, by adding a term proportional to the sum of their squares or absolute values. A feature that happens to be recorded in small units needs a large coefficient to have any effect at all, and the regularization penalty then punishes that feature simply for the units it arrived in, independent of how useful it actually is. Skip scaling before a regularized linear model and you're not testing which features matter; you're testing which features happen to be measured in small numbers. Notebook 8 returns to this properly.

Model familyNeeds scaling?Why
k-nearest neighbours, k-means, SVMs, PCA, neural networksYesDistance or gradient calculations treat large-magnitude columns as more important by default
Ridge, Lasso, and other regularized linear modelsYesThe penalty term punishes coefficient size, which is entangled with a column's units
Decision trees, random forests, gradient boostingNoThreshold splits only depend on ordering, which scaling always preserves

Scaling costs essentially nothing to apply, which is the practical resolution to all of this: when a pipeline has to serve more than one model family, or you're not yet certain which one will win, including a scaler by default is a safe habit rather than a wasted step.

4Scalers are transformers, so the leakage rule applies again

The third notebook in a row to hit the identical rule from a different direction

Notebook 1 established this rule for a scaler feeding a logistic regression, and notebook 2 established it again for an imputer's median. A scaler learns a mean, a standard deviation, a minimum, a maximum, or a set of quartiles directly from whatever data it's shown, and every one of those learned numbers has to come from training rows only. California Housing's outlier-heavy columns make the cost of skipping that rule easy to see.

The reason is exactly the outliers section 2 already introduced: where a handful of extreme values happen to land when a random split is drawn has an outsized effect on any statistic computed from them, which means the columns most in need of careful, correct scaling are precisely the ones where fitting on the wrong rows does the most damage.

The fix hasn't changed since notebook 1: put the scaler inside a pipeline, and let it call fit on training data alone.

The rule underneath all three notebooks

This is the third notebook running where the identical rule has appeared, first for an imputer, now for a scaler, and it returns again in notebook 4 for encoders. The repetition is deliberate rather than padding: anything with a fit method learns something from whatever data you show it, and everything it learns has to come from the training set alone. Hold onto that one sentence and you never need a separate memorized list of which specific preprocessing steps are safe.

5Non-linear transformations for skewed data

Scaling repositions a distribution. It cannot reshape one. This section is about the tools that can

Skew matters because most models, and nearly every summary statistic anyone reports from one, work best when a column is roughly symmetric. In a strongly right-skewed column, the mean sits well above the median, a small number of extreme rows dominate any objective built on squared error, and a linear model is forced to fit a slope that compromises between the tail and the bulk of ordinary values, doing an honest job of describing neither.

Three transformations, in order of how hard they compress a long tail:

  • Square root compresses gently. It handles zero without complaint but not negative values.
  • Log compresses more aggressively, and is the usual first choice for money, counts, and durations. Undefined at zero, which is why log1p, computing log(1 + x) rather than log(x), is the version that shows up in practice.
  • Reciprocal is aggressive to the point of being dangerous, and it reverses the order of every value along the way. Rarely what you actually want.
Four candidates, one column, one number to compare them by
income_values = housing['MedInc'].values

print('Median income, skew after each transformation:')

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(17, 3.8))

for ax, (name, values) in zip(axes, candidates.items()):
    ax.hist(values, bins=60, color='#3b76af', edgecolor='white')
    ax.set(title=f'{name}\nskew {pd.Series(values).skew():.3f}', xlabel='value')

axes[0].set_ylabel('block groups')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
log1p produces the closest thing to a symmetric bell shape here. The reciprocal panel shows exactly how compressing a tail too hard just builds a new one.

The reciprocal moved skew from 1.647 to 2.448, worse than doing nothing at all, because compressing the tail that aggressively manufactures a brand new tail on the opposite side. It also inverts the ordering of every value it touches: the highest income in the dataset becomes the smallest number after transformation, which quietly flips the sign of every relationship that column takes part in afterward. Anyone reading a coefficient on the transformed version without remembering this would draw the exact opposite conclusion from the correct one.

The habit this failure should build

More compression is not automatically better. The goal of a transformation like this is symmetry, not the smallest numbers you can produce, and the only way to know whether a transformation actually helped is to check the resulting skew rather than assume that applying a stronger operation must have done more good.

Box-Cox and Yeo-Johnson

Trying square root, then log, then comparing the results by hand raises an obvious question: why guess at all, when you could search for the exact transformation that makes a column as symmetric as possible? That's precisely what the power transforms do. Both belong to a family parameterized by a single number, conventionally called lambda, and instead of committing to a specific square root or log, they search for the lambda that brings a column closest to a normal distribution.

The Box-Cox transform is defined piecewise:

x(λ) = (xλ − 1) / λ   if λ ≠ 0,     log(x)   if λ = 0

Different values of lambda recover transformations already familiar by this point: a lambda of 1 leaves the data essentially untouched, 0.5 behaves like a square root, 0 behaves like a log, and −1 behaves like a reciprocal. Rather than picking one of these by hand, Box-Cox fits the value of lambda directly from the data.

Letting the data choose its own transformation
income_2d = housing[['MedInc']]

box_cox = PowerTransformer(method='box-cox').fit(income_2d)
yeo = PowerTransformer(method='yeo-johnson').fit(income_2d)

bc_values = box_cox.transform(income_2d).ravel()
yj_values = yeo.transform(income_2d).ravel()

print(f'Original skew          : {housing["MedInc"].skew():7.3f}')
print(f'Best manual (log1p)    : {pd.Series(np.log1p(income_values)).skew():7.3f}')
print()
print(f'Box-Cox     lambda = {box_cox.lambdas_[0]:7.4f}  ->  skew {pd.Series(bc_values).skew():7.3f}')
print(f'Yeo-Johnson lambda = {yeo.lambdas_[0]:7.4f}  ->  skew {pd.Series(yj_values).skew():7.3f}')
Output
Original skew          :   1.647
Best manual (log1p)    :   0.226

Box-Cox     lambda =  0.0909  ->  skew   0.005
Yeo-Johnson lambda = -0.1985  ->  skew  -0.003

Box-Cox independently arrived at a lambda of 0.0909, close enough to zero that it essentially rediscovered "something very close to a log transform" on its own, without being told that log was a reasonable place to look. It reached a skew of 0.005 against the manually chosen log transform's 0.226, a meaningfully closer approach to perfect symmetry, purely because it's searching a continuous space of possible transformations rather than testing a handful of named options.

The formula itself hides a real constraint. Both log(x) and fractional powers of x require strictly positive input, so Box-Cox inherits that requirement whole. Longitude, which runs negative across all of California, puts that constraint to an immediate, practical test.

What happens when the constraint is violated
longitude = housing[['Longitude']]
print(f'Longitude range: {longitude.values.min():.2f} to {longitude.values.max():.2f}')
print()

try:
    PowerTransformer(method='box-cox').fit(longitude)
    print('Box-Cox succeeded')
except ValueError as err:
    print(f'Box-Cox raises  : ValueError: {err}')

yeo_lon = PowerTransformer(method='yeo-johnson').fit(longitude)
print(f'Yeo-Johnson     : fits fine, lambda = {yeo_lon.lambdas_[0]:.4f}')
Output
Longitude range: -124.35 to -114.31

Box-Cox raises  : ValueError: The Box-Cox transformation can only be applied to strictly positive data
Yeo-Johnson     : fits fine, lambda = 8.0189

That error is not a bug to code around. It's the method correctly telling you it doesn't apply to this column, which is a more useful outcome than silently returning something wrong. Yeo-Johnson exists precisely to lift the constraint: it extends the same underlying idea with a modified formula that also accepts zero and negative values, which is why it, rather than Box-Cox, is the default method in scikit-learn's PowerTransformer.

A practical rule for choosing between them

Use Box-Cox only when every value in the column is strictly positive. Use Yeo-Johnson everywhere else. Shifting a column upward by a constant purely to make Box-Cox accept it is technically possible and rarely worth doing, since the shift you pick is itself an arbitrary choice that changes the lambda the method ultimately fits. And keep in mind that neither power transform is automatically superior to a plain log: both fit an extra parameter from training data, which is one more thing that has to be learned honestly rather than leaked from elsewhere, and a plain log transform is far easier to explain to someone reviewing your work later.

All of this has been measured in skew, a useful proxy but not the actual target. Does correcting skew change what a downstream model can actually do?

Feeding a regression model the raw columns, then the log-transformed ones, then Yeo-Johnson on everything

6Binning, and when it costs more than it gives

An honest comparison, including the result most tutorials introducing this technique would rather not print

Binning converts a continuous column into a set of ranges: ages 0 to 17, 18 to 34, 35 to 54, and upward. The appeal is real. Each bin becomes its own indicator column with its own coefficient, which lets a plain linear model assign a completely different effect to each range without needing a single straight line to fit all of them at once, the same curvature problem notebook 1 first raised.

Three ways to choose where the bin edges fall:

  • Equal width cuts the column's range into intervals of identical size, via pd.cut. Simple to reason about, and it produces badly unbalanced bins the moment the underlying data is skewed.
  • Equal frequency cuts so each bin holds the same number of rows, via pd.qcut. Balanced counts, at the cost of edges that land on values with no obvious meaning.
  • Domain-driven uses boundaries that mean something to the people using the model, such as a contract's renewal points.

Notebook 1 already found that churn against tenure is strongly curved, and addressed it by engineering log_tenure and tenure_squared. Binning is the alternative solution to that exact same curvature problem, which makes the two directly comparable on identical data.

The comparison that actually decides whether any of this was worth doing

This is the result most introductions to binning skip past. Equal-width binning performs worse than doing nothing at all. Five uniform bins score an AUC of 0.8038 against raw tenure's 0.8087. Real effort went in, four new columns came out, and the result landed behind where it started.

The smooth transformation from notebook 1 wins outright, at 0.8159. Quantile binning with ten bins comes close behind at 0.8155, but needs ten columns to get there where the smooth version needed exactly two. The reason traces back to what binning does by design: it throws information away on purpose. A customer at 3 months of tenure and one at 13 months can land in the same equal-width bin and become completely indistinguishable to the model, even though notebook 1 already showed churn risk falling from 56.8% to roughly 30% across almost that exact span. Equal-frequency binning does better precisely because it places more edges where the data is dense, but it still discards whatever ordering existed within each bin it creates.

When binning genuinely earns its place

When the underlying relationship is a real step rather than a smooth curve, a tax bracket or a price tier where crossing a specific threshold actually changes behavior. When the result has to be explained to someone non-technical: "customers in their first six months" is something a retention team can act on directly, in a way a log-transformed coefficient never will be. When there's enough data that losing within-bin detail costs little, and enough noise that the smoothing genuinely helps rather than hides signal. And when limiting how finely a model can fit the training data is the actual goal, functioning as a deliberate form of regularization. Outside of these, prefer quantile or domain-driven edges over equal width, and always check the result against the untouched column rather than assuming the extra work paid for itself.

7Polynomial and interaction features

Where the order two operations happen in turns the single worst result in this notebook into the single best one

Notebook 1 made the underlying case for this technique: a linear model can only ever draw straight boundaries, and multiplying two columns together lets that same linear model express a curve it otherwise couldn't reach. That was done by hand, for one engineered feature. PolynomialFeatures performs the identical idea exhaustively, across every column at once.

For two inputs a and b at degree 2, it generates a, b, a², ab, and b². The ab term is the interaction: it captures the idea that the effect of one variable can depend on the level of another. The two squared terms are what let the model bend.

The expansion on two toy rows, small enough to read completely

Same columns, same model, same random seed. Squaring first, then scaling, produced R-squared of −11.16. Taking the log first, then squaring, then scaling, reached 0.6505, the largest single gain anywhere in this notebook. Nothing about the data or the model changed between those two runs. Only the sequence did, and that sequence is worth stating as a rule rather than a one-off observation: transform skew before expanding polynomially, always, because squaring an outlier squares the problem it already was.

Two more findings from that same result table are worth pulling out on their own.

Degree 3 is worse than degree 2, not better. It reaches 0.6294 using 83 columns, against degree 2's 0.6505 from only 27. More expansion stopped helping and started actively hurting, because 83 heavily correlated columns give the model enough freedom to start fitting noise specific to whichever rows happened to land in each training fold. Polynomial degree is a hyperparameter to search over and validate, not a dial that's safe to simply turn up whenever more seems better.

Interaction terms alone don't capture everything here. Restricting the expansion to interactions only, dropping every squared term, reaches 0.6122 from 21 columns, short of the full expansion's 0.6505. The squared terms were carrying genuine signal on their own, which lines up with the underlying reality that the relationship between income and house value really does curve rather than run straight.

The cost that scales fastest here is dimensionality, and it's worth having the actual arithmetic rather than a vague sense that "it grows." Degree 2 on 6 columns produces 27. Degree 3 on the same 6 produces 83. Degree 2 on 100 columns would produce 5,150, since the count of second-degree terms grows roughly with the square of the number of features you start with. PolynomialFeatures is consequently a tool suited to small, carefully chosen feature sets. Once a dataset has grown to dozens or hundreds of columns, hand-built interactions, chosen because you have a specific reason to expect them, become the more practical choice.

In practice the two approaches complement rather than compete with each other. Hand-built features encode what you already know going in. Polynomial expansion searches for structure you didn't think to look for. Starting with the first, and reaching for the second only once you've genuinely exhausted your own ideas, is a reasonable order to work in.

Key things to remember from this notebook

Scaling makes existing information usable. It doesn't add any. TotalCharges held 99.97% of the variance across Telco's three numeric columns, so four genuinely predictive binary flags moved an unscaled KNN from 0.7846 to 0.7848, essentially nothing. Those same flags score 0.8022 on their own, and scaling the full seven-column set lifted AUC to 0.8272. Nothing errors when this goes wrong, which is exactly why it's easy to miss in a real project.

Pick a scaler by asking what statistics it's built from. MinMaxScaler is defined entirely by the two most extreme values in a column, so one household of 1,243 people compressed 99% of California's neighborhoods into the range 0.001 to 0.004. RobustScaler, built from the median and quartiles, gave the identical data a sensible spread instead. StandardScaler remains the reasonable default; reach for RobustScaler when outliers are staying on purpose, and MinMaxScaler only when a bounded range is genuinely required.

Scaling changes units, not shape. All three scalers left median income's skew at exactly 1.647, because a linear rescaling cannot reshape a distribution. Fixing shape needs a non-linear transformation instead.

Know which models actually care. A random forest scored identically with and without scaling, since its splits depend only on ordering, never magnitude. Logistic regression matched it on this data too, but converged in 11 iterations instead of 54, and produced coefficients that could finally be compared against one another.

Check that a transformation actually helped, rather than assuming a stronger one must have. The reciprocal made median income worse, pushing skew from 1.647 to 2.448 while silently reversing the order of every value. Log reached 0.226, and Box-Cox found 0.005 by fitting its own exponent directly from the data. Box-Cox requires strictly positive input and correctly refuses to run on California's negative longitudes, which is exactly why Yeo-Johnson is scikit-learn's default.

Binning is not free. Equal-width bins on tenure scored 0.8038 against 0.8087 for the column left untouched, meaning the extra work made the model measurably worse. Notebook 1's log-and-square combination reached 0.8159 with two columns where ten-bin quantile binning needed five times as many columns to reach 0.8155. Bin for explainability or genuine step behavior, never as a reflex, and favor quantile or domain edges over equal width when you do.

Order of operations is not a minor detail. PolynomialFeatures on raw housing data produced an R-squared of −11.16, worse than predicting the mean, because squaring a population of 35,682 produces 1.27 billion and no amount of scaling or regularization recovers from that afterward. The identical expansion applied after a log transform reached 0.6505, the largest gain anywhere in this notebook. Degree 3 then fell back to 0.6294, confirming that more expansion is not automatically better expansion.

Notebook 4 turns to categorical variables, where the central problem changes shape: models need numbers, categories are labels, and every method of turning one into the other makes a claim about the data that can turn out right or wrong.
