    Start hereIntroduction#

    The first two notebooks fixed broken things: a customer ID that shouldn't have been a feature, a blank cell hiding as text, a missing age. This one is different. Every number here is correct. Nothing is mistyped or missing. Yet some of these correct numbers will make a model do worse than if it ignored the data completely.

    That's because a model sees your numbers through its own assumptions, and those assumptions are rarely stated. K-nearest neighbours assumes distance means the same thing in every column. A linear model assumes a straight line fits the relationship. Squaring a column assumes it has no extreme values, because squaring makes them even more extreme. When these assumptions are broken, nothing errors. The model just gets worse.

    Notebook 2 already gave two previews of this. Its KNN imputer had to scale the columns first, or Fare would have controlled every distance. And log1p brought Fare's skew down from 4.79 to 0.39 without deleting a row. This notebook explains both properly.

    It closes the gap between what your numbers look like and what your model needs, using four kinds of transformation:

    • scaling, so no column dominates just because it uses bigger numbers
    • non-linear transforms, to fix columns that are heavily skewed
    • binning, which turns a number into ranges, and when that's worth doing
    • polynomial features, which let a model follow a curve

    The two datasets#

    Telco comes back from the first two notebooks for the classification work. This notebook also adds California Housing, a 20,640-row census dataset that comes with scikit-learn. It gives us a regression target and, more importantly, columns that are badly skewed, which Telco's rarely are.

    Two things to know about it. Each row is a census block group, a neighborhood of roughly a few hundred to a few thousand people, so columns like AveRooms are averages, not one house. And some columns were capped when the data was made: median income stops at 15 (in tens of thousands of dollars), house age at 52 years, and the target, median house value in hundreds of thousands, at 5, or $500,000.

    Section 1Why scaling matters#

    A real, measurable way that useful features get ignored

    The usual explanation is that "some algorithms are sensitive to scale." That's true but not very helpful, because it doesn't tell you when it matters or how much. Telco's numeric columns show the problem clearly enough to measure.

    Distance-based algorithms, especially k-nearest neighbours, decide which rows are similar by measuring how far apart they are. Euclidean distance takes the difference in each column, squares it, and adds them up. Because of the squaring, a column with big numbers adds a lot to the total and a column with small numbers adds very little. That happens no matter which column is actually more useful.

    Section 2The three scalers, and what each does to an outlier#

    All three put columns on a similar scale, but they define "similar" differently

    Three formulas, each making columns comparable in a different way:

    StandardScaler subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, so every column ends up with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

    z = (x − μ) / σ

    MinMaxScaler squeezes a column into a fixed range, 0 to 1 by default.

    x' = (x − xmin) / (xmax − xmin)

    RobustScaler subtracts the median and divides by the interquartile range.

    x' = (x − median) / (Q3 − Q1)

    The formulas already tell you how each one handles extreme values. Notebook 2 showed that the mean and standard deviation get pulled around by a few huge values, when three Fare values moved the z-score cutoff by 27 pounds. StandardScaler uses the mean and standard deviation. MinMaxScaler uses the two most extreme values, which makes it the most fragile. RobustScaler uses the median and quartiles, which barely move no matter how extreme a few outliers are.

    California Housing's AveOccup column (average people per household in each census block) is a perfect test. The median is a normal 2.82 people, but the maximum is 1,243.33, most likely a place like a dormitory or institution, where many people are counted but very few households.

    PythonThe same column, through all three scalers
    occupancy = housing[['AveOccup']]
print(f'AveOccup: min {occupancy.values.min():.2f}, median {np.median(occupancy):.2f}, max {occupancy.values.max():.2f}')
print()

rows = []
for name, scaler in [('StandardScaler', StandardScaler()),
                     ('MinMaxScaler', MinMaxScaler()),
                     ('RobustScaler', RobustScaler())]:
    scaled = scaler.fit_transform(occupancy).ravel()
    q1, q3 = np.percentile(scaled, [25, 75])
    lo, hi = np.percentile(scaled, [0.5, 99.5])
    rows.append({
        'scaler': name,
        'min': scaled.min(),
        'median': np.median(scaled),
        'max': scaled.max(),
        'IQR_width': q3 - q1,
        'middle_99pct_spans': f'[{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]',
    })

StandardScaler -0.2290 -0.0243  119.4191     0.0821    [-0.156, 0.294]
  MinMaxScaler  0.0000  0.0017    1.0000     0.0007     [0.001, 0.004]
  RobustScaler -2.4936  0.0000 1455.1161     1.0000    [-1.608, 3.876]

    Focus on the IQR_width column. It shows how much room the normal neighborhoods have after scaling.

    MinMaxScaler fails badly here. The middle 99% of neighborhoods gets squeezed into the range 0.001 to 0.004. The middle half of neighborhoods differ by almost a whole person per household, yet they now fit in a range just 0.0007 wide. To a distance-based model like KNN, they look almost identical. One far-away outlier sets the top of the scale for everyone.

    StandardScaler does okay, but the outlier still hurts. The middle 99% spans about 0.45 units, which is tighter than it should be, because the extreme value inflates the standard deviation everyone is divided by.

    RobustScaler isn't affected. Its interquartile width is 1.0 by design, and the middle 99% spans about 5.5 units, which is plenty of room. The outlier still sits far out at 1,455, but it doesn't squash everyone else.

    Which one to use

    StandardScaler is the default. Use it unless you have a reason not to. Use RobustScaler when the column has outliers you've decided to keep, which is common in financial and behavioral data. Use MinMaxScaler only when you need a fixed range, like pixel values or a network that expects 0 to 1, and check for outliers first, since they break it most easily.

    It's easy to assume scaling changes the shape of the data. Let's check: does it change the shape, or only the units?

    This is the most useful fact in this section, and the most often confused: scaling changes units, not shape. A skewed column stays just as skewed after scaling. Fixing shape is a different job, covered in section 5.

    Section 3Which algorithms actually need scaling#

    Many projects scale every numeric column by habit. Seeing where it does nothing shows what it's really for

    Trees are the easiest case. A decision tree asks questions like "is TotalCharges above 1,400?" If you multiply every value by 1,000, subtract the mean or squeeze it into 0 to 1, the threshold number changes, but the same rows still land on each side. Rescaling can only relabel the threshold. It can't change which rows are above or below it.

    PythonTesting that directly: a random forest, raw and scaled
    forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=120, random_state=RANDOM_STATE, n_jobs=1)

forest_raw = churn_auc(forest)
forest_scaled = churn_auc(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), forest))

print(f'RandomForest on raw features : AUC {forest_raw:.4f}')
print(f'RandomForest + StandardScaler: AUC {forest_scaled:.4f}')
print(f'Difference                   : {abs(forest_scaled - forest_raw):.4f}')
    Output
    RandomForest on raw features : AUC 0.8012
RandomForest + StandardScaler: AUC 0.8020
Difference                   : 0.0008

    A difference of 0.0008 is just noise. In perfect math the two numbers would be identical, because a tree only compares values to thresholds and scaling keeps the order the same. The tiny gap comes from computer rounding: rescaled values give slightly different split points, and a tie between two equally good splits can go a different way. With a different random seed, the gap could flip direction.

    So for tree models, scaling does nothing. But it also costs nothing, so it's fine to include a scaler in your pipeline even if today's model is a tree. Tomorrow's model might not be.

    PythonLogistic regression: same AUC, but not the same everything
    raw_model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000).fit(telco_numeric, churned)

                             telco_numeric, churned, cv=cv, scoring='roc_auc').mean()

print(f'LogisticRegression scaled : AUC {scaled_auc:.4f}   converged in {scaled_model.named_steps["logisticregression"].n_iter_[0]} iterations')
print()
print('Coefficients:')

    'raw': raw_model.coef_[0],
    'scaled': scaled_model.named_steps['logisticregression'].coef_[0],
}, index=telco_numeric.columns).round(5).to_string())

                    raw   scaled
tenure         -0.06711 -1.61603
MonthlyCharges  0.03020  0.91280
TotalCharges    0.00015  0.29866

    Here the AUC doesn't change at all: 0.8087 either way on these three columns. That doesn't mean scaling was pointless, because two other things changed that a single score won't show.

    The model needed 11 steps to converge instead of 54. When one column's scale is much bigger than the others, the optimizer behind logistic regression has to zigzag through a long, narrow valley to find the answer. Scaling makes that valley round, so it gets there faster.

    On three columns and 7,000 rows, a few extra steps don't matter. On a wide dataset with a slower solver, the model can hit its step limit before it finishes. Scikit-learn then shows a ConvergenceWarning, and the model you get is only partly trained.

    The coefficients became comparable. In the unscaled model, TotalCharges has a coefficient of 0.00015 and tenure has −0.06711. It's tempting to think the first is less important because it's smaller. That's wrong. One is "per dollar of total charges" and the other is "per month of tenure," so you can't compare them directly. After scaling, the coefficients become −1.616, 0.913 and 0.299, all in the same unit (one standard deviation), so their sizes can now be compared.

    One caution: tenure and TotalCharges rise together, so the model splits credit between them in ways that are hard to read. Comparable units don't make coefficients a clean measure of importance.

    There's a third reason, and it explains why the AUC didn't move. Scikit-learn's LogisticRegression actually applies a small penalty by default, but with 7,000 rows it's too weak to matter here. Ridge and Lasso regression penalize large coefficients. A feature measured in small units needs a large coefficient to have any effect, so the penalty punishes it just because of its units, not because it's less useful.

    If you skip scaling before a regularized linear model, you're not testing which features matter. You're testing which ones happen to use small numbers. Notebook 8 covers this in more detail.

    Model familyNeeds scaling?Why
    k-nearest neighbours, k-means, SVMs, PCA, neural networksYesDistance and gradient calculations treat columns with big numbers as more important
    Ridge, Lasso and other regularized linear modelsYesThe penalty depends on coefficient size, which depends on a column's units
    Decision trees, random forests, gradient boostingNoSplits only depend on the order of values, and scaling keeps the order

    Plain linear and logistic regression sit in between. Without a penalty, scaling doesn't change their predictions, but it still speeds up training and makes coefficients comparable, as you just saw.

    Scaling costs almost nothing. So when a pipeline might serve more than one type of model, or you don't yet know which model will win, adding a scaler by default is a safe habit.

    Section 4Scalers are transformers, so the leakage rule applies again#

    The third notebook in a row where the same rule comes up

    Notebook 1 set this rule for a scaler, and notebook 2 applied it again to an imputer's median. A scaler learns a mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum or quartiles from the data it sees, and all of those must come from training rows only. California Housing's outliers make the cost of breaking this rule easy to see.

    The reason is the outliers from section 2. Where a few extreme values land in a random split has a big effect on any statistic calculated from them. So the columns that most need careful scaling are also the ones where fitting on the wrong rows changes the numbers most.

    The effect on a final score is usually small, and notebook 9 measures it. The reason to follow the rule anyway is that the same habit protects you from leaks that are big, which later notebooks show.

    The fix is the same as in notebook 1: put the scaler inside a pipeline and let it fit on training data only.

    The rule behind all three notebooks

    This is the third notebook in a row with the same rule: a scaler in notebook 1, an imputer in notebook 2, and a scaler again here. Notebook 4 applies it to encoders. The repetition is on purpose: anything with a fit method learns from the data you give it, and everything it learns must come from the training set only. Remember that one sentence, and you don't need a list of which steps are safe.

    Section 5Non-linear transformations for skewed data#

    Scaling moves a distribution. It can't change its shape. This section covers the tools that can

    Skew matters for some models, not all. A common myth is that linear models need normally distributed inputs. They don't. The real problems are these: in a strongly right-skewed column, a few extreme rows pull hard on the fitted line, and the relationship with the target is often closer to a straight line after a log. A linear model has to fit one slope that covers both the tail and the normal values, and it ends up describing neither well.

    Tree models don't care about skew, because a log keeps the order of values, and trees only use order.

    Three transformations, from gentlest to strongest:

    • Square root compresses gently. It works with zero but not with negative values.
    • Log compresses more strongly and is the usual first choice for money, counts and durations. It doesn't work at zero, so in practice people use log1p, which calculates log(1 + x) instead of log(x).
    • Reciprocal (1/x) is so strong it's risky, and it reverses the order of all values. It's rarely what you want.
    PythonFour candidates, one column, one number to compare them by
    income_values = housing['MedInc'].values

candidates = {
    'original': income_values,
    'sqrt': np.sqrt(income_values),
    'log1p': np.log1p(income_values),
    'reciprocal': 1 / income_values,
}

print('Median income, skew after each transformation:')
for name, values in candidates.items():

for ax, (name, values) in zip(axes, candidates.items()):
    ax.hist(values, bins=60, color='#3b76af', edgecolor='white')
    ax.set(title=f'{name}\nskew {pd.Series(values).skew():.3f}', xlabel='value')

axes[0].set_ylabel('block groups')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

    The reciprocal made skew worse, from 1.647 to 2.448, because squeezing the tail that hard creates a new tail on the other side. It also flips the order of every value: the highest income becomes the smallest number. That reverses the direction of every relationship the column is part of. If you read a coefficient on the transformed column and forgot this, you'd reach the opposite conclusion.

    The lesson

    Stronger isn't automatically better. The goal is a balanced shape, not the smallest numbers. The only way to know if a transformation helped is to check the skew afterward.

    Box-Cox and Yeo-Johnson#

    Trying square root, then log, and comparing by hand raises a question: why guess, when you could search for the transformation that makes a column most balanced? That's what power transforms do. They use a single number called lambda and search for the lambda that makes the column look as close to a normal distribution as possible.

    The Box-Cox transform is defined like this:

    x(λ) = (xλ − 1) / λ   if λ ≠ 0,     log(x)   if λ = 0

    Different lambda values give transformations you already know: 1 leaves the data almost unchanged, 0.5 acts like a square root, 0 acts like a log and −1 acts like a reciprocal. Box-Cox picks the best lambda from the data instead of you choosing one.

    PythonLetting the data choose its own transformation
    income_2d = housing[['MedInc']]

box_cox = PowerTransformer(method='box-cox').fit(income_2d)
yeo = PowerTransformer(method='yeo-johnson').fit(income_2d)

bc_values = box_cox.transform(income_2d).ravel()
yj_values = yeo.transform(income_2d).ravel()

print(f'Original skew          : {housing["MedInc"].skew():7.3f}')
print(f'Best manual (log1p)    : {pd.Series(np.log1p(income_values)).skew():7.3f}')
print()
print(f'Box-Cox     lambda = {box_cox.lambdas_[0]:7.4f}  ->  skew {pd.Series(bc_values).skew():7.3f}')
print(f'Yeo-Johnson lambda = {yeo.lambdas_[0]:7.4f}  ->  skew {pd.Series(yj_values).skew():7.3f}')
    Output
    Original skew          :   1.647
    Best manual (log1p)    :   0.226

Box-Cox     lambda =  0.0909  ->  skew   0.005
Yeo-Johnson lambda = -0.1985  ->  skew  -0.003

    Box-Cox picked a lambda of 0.0909, very close to zero. So on its own, it found something very close to a log transform. It reached a skew of 0.005, compared with 0.226 for the hand-picked log, because it searches all possible values instead of trying a few named options.

    One detail that matters later: scikit-learn's PowerTransformer also standardizes its output to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 by default. So a pipeline that uses it doesn't need a separate scaler.

    The formula has a limit. Both log(x) and fractional powers need values above zero, so Box-Cox does too. Longitude, which is negative everywhere in California, tests this right away.

    PythonWhat happens when the constraint is violated
    longitude = housing[['Longitude']]
print(f'Longitude range: {longitude.values.min():.2f} to {longitude.values.max():.2f}')
print()

try:
    PowerTransformer(method='box-cox').fit(longitude)
    print('Box-Cox succeeded')
except ValueError as err:
    print(f'Box-Cox raises  : ValueError: {err}')

yeo_lon = PowerTransformer(method='yeo-johnson').fit(longitude)
print(f'Yeo-Johnson     : fits fine, lambda = {yeo_lon.lambdas_[0]:.4f}')
    Output
    Longitude range: -124.35 to -114.31

Box-Cox raises  : ValueError: The Box-Cox transformation can only be applied to strictly positive data
    Yeo-Johnson     : fits fine, lambda = 8.0189

    That error isn't a bug to work around. The method is correctly telling you it doesn't work on this column, which is better than quietly returning something wrong. Yeo-Johnson was made to remove this limit. It uses a modified formula that also accepts zero and negative values, which is why it's the default in scikit-learn's PowerTransformer.

    But notice the lambda it picked: 8.0. That's an extreme reshaping, applied to a column with a skew of only −0.30 that never needed it. Running without an error isn't the same as being the right choice.

    Which one to use

    Use Box-Cox only when every value is above zero. Use Yeo-Johnson for everything else. You could shift a column up by a constant so Box-Cox accepts it, but it's rarely worth it, because the shift you pick changes the lambda.

    Also, neither power transform is always better than a plain log. Both learn an extra number from training data, which is one more thing that must not leak. And a plain log is much easier to explain to someone reviewing your work.

    So far we've measured skew, which is useful but not the real goal. Does fixing skew actually help a model?

    PythonFeeding a regression model the raw columns, then the log-transformed ones, then Yeo-Johnson on everything
    housing_features = ['MedInc', 'HouseAge', 'AveRooms', 'AveBedrms', 'Population', 'AveOccup']
skewed = ['MedInc', 'AveRooms', 'AveBedrms', 'Population', 'AveOccup']   # HouseAge is already symmetric

raw_features = housing[housing_features]
logged_features = raw_features.copy()
logged_features[skewed] = np.log1p(logged_features[skewed])

cv_reg = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=RANDOM_STATE)

def housing_r2(model, X):
    return cross_val_score(model, X, house_value, cv=cv_reg, scoring='r2').mean()


print(f"raw features                 R2 = {housing_r2(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), Ridge()), raw_features):.4f}")
print(f"log1p on the 5 skewed cols   R2 = {housing_r2(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), Ridge()), logged_features):.4f}")
print(f"Yeo-Johnson on all columns   R2 = {housing_r2(make_pipeline(PowerTransformer(method='yeo-johnson'), Ridge()), raw_features):.4f}")
    Output
    raw features                 R2 = 0.5348
log1p on the 5 skewed cols   R2 = 0.5699
    Yeo-Johnson on all columns   R2 = 0.5676

    R-squared goes from 0.5348 to 0.5699. The transformed features explain about three and a half more percentage points of the variation in house prices, just from changing the shape of five columns. Yeo-Johnson applied to all six columns automatically scores 0.5676, about the same as the hand-picked logs, without anyone deciding which columns needed it. (These models leave out Latitude and Longitude on purpose. Location matters a lot for house prices, but not in a straight line, so it needs different tools.)

    To be clear, Yeo-Johnson matched the careful manual work but didn't beat it. It also transformed HouseAge, which has a skew of 0.06 and never needed changing. And its pipeline had no separate scaler, because PowerTransformer standardizes its output by default.

    Section 6Binning, and when it costs more than it gives#

    An honest comparison, including the result most tutorials don't show

    Binning turns a continuous number into ranges, like ages 0 to 17, 18 to 34, 35 to 54 and so on. The appeal is real. Each bin becomes its own column with its own coefficient, so a simple linear model can give each range a different effect. It doesn't need one straight line to fit them all. That's the same curve problem notebook 1 raised.

    Three ways to choose where bins start and end:

    • Equal width splits the range into same-size intervals, using pd.cut. It's easy to understand but gives very uneven bins when the data is skewed.
    • Equal frequency splits so each bin has the same number of rows, using pd.qcut. The counts are balanced, but the edges land on numbers with no real meaning.
    • Domain-based uses edges that mean something to the people using the model, like a contract's renewal dates.

    Notebook 1 found that churn against tenure is strongly curved, and fixed it with log_tenure and tenure_squared. Binning is another way to handle the same curve, so we can compare the two on the same data.

    PythonThe same column, cut two different ways
    tenure = telco['tenure']

equal_width = pd.cut(tenure, bins=5)
equal_freq = pd.qcut(tenure, q=5)

comparison = pd.DataFrame({
    'equal_width_counts': equal_width.value_counts().sort_index().values,
    'equal_width_edges': [str(i) for i in equal_width.value_counts().sort_index().index],
    'equal_freq_counts': equal_freq.value_counts().sort_index().values,
    'equal_freq_edges': [str(i) for i in equal_freq.value_counts().sort_index().index],
})

print('Same column, same number of bins, very different memberships:')
print(comparison.to_string(index=False))
    Output

    PythonThe comparison that actually decides whether any of this was worth doing
    other_numeric = telco[['MonthlyCharges', 'TotalCharges']]   # tenure deliberately left out, we re-add it below

def logistic_auc(X):
    return cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000)),
                           X, churned, cv=cv, scoring='roc_auc').mean()


results = {}
results['raw tenure'] = logistic_auc(telco_numeric)
results['log + square (notebook 1)'] = logistic_auc(
    telco_numeric.assign(log_tenure=np.log1p(tenure), tenure_squared=tenure ** 2))

for strategy in ['uniform', 'quantile']:
    for n_bins in [5, 10]:
        kwargs = dict(n_bins=n_bins, encode='onehot-dense', strategy=strategy)
        if strategy == 'quantile':
            kwargs['quantile_method'] = 'averaged_inverted_cdf'
        binned = pd.DataFrame(KBinsDiscretizer(**kwargs).fit_transform(telco[['tenure']]),
                              index=telco.index).add_prefix('bin_')
        results[f'binned: {strategy}, {n_bins} bins'] = logistic_auc(
            pd.concat([other_numeric, binned], axis=1))

domain_bins = pd.cut(tenure, [0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72], labels=['0-6m', '6-12m', '1-2y', '2-4y', '4y+'])
results['binned: domain, 5 bins'] = logistic_auc(
    pd.concat([other_numeric, pd.get_dummies(domain_bins, prefix='ten').astype(int)], axis=1))

print('How to represent tenure, ranked by ROC AUC:')
print(scores.round(4).to_string())
    Output
    How to represent tenure, ranked by ROC AUC:

binned: quantile, 10 bins    0.8155

binned: domain, 5 bins       0.8097

    Most introductions to binning skip this result. Equal-width binning did worse than doing nothing. Five equal bins scored an AUC of 0.8038, against 0.8087 for raw tenure. We did extra work, added four columns, and ended up worse off.

    The smooth transformation from notebook 1 wins, at 0.8159. Ten quantile bins come close at 0.8155, but need ten columns where the smooth version used three (tenure, its log and its square). The reason is that binning throws information away on purpose.

    A customer at 3 months and one at 13 months can land in the same equal-width bin and look identical to the model, even though in notebook 1's data, churn is 56.8% in the first three months and 28.7% for customers between one and two years. Equal-frequency binning does better because it puts more edges where there's more data, but it still loses the order within each bin.

    When binning is worth it

    Binning makes sense when the relationship really is a step, not a smooth curve, like a tax bracket or a price tier where crossing a line changes behavior. It also helps when you need to explain results to non-technical people: "customers in their first six months" is something a team can act on, and a log-transformed coefficient isn't.

    It works when you have lots of data, so losing detail inside each bin costs little, and enough noise that smoothing helps. And it can be a deliberate way to stop a model from fitting training data too closely. Otherwise, use quantile or domain-based edges instead of equal width, and always compare against the original column.

    Section 7Polynomial and interaction features#

    Changing the order of two steps turns the worst result in this notebook into the best one

    Notebook 1 made the case for this: a linear model can only draw straight lines, and multiplying two columns lets it draw a curve. We did that by hand for one feature. PolynomialFeatures does the same thing automatically, for every column at once.

    For two inputs a and b at degree 2, it creates a, b, a², ab and b². The ab term is the interaction: it captures how the effect of one variable can depend on the other. The squared terms let the model bend.

    PythonThe expansion on two toy rows, small enough to read completely
    tiny = pd.DataFrame({'income': [3.0, 5.0], 'rooms': [4.0, 6.0]})

poly_demo = PolynomialFeatures(degree=2, include_bias=False)
expanded = poly_demo.fit_transform(tiny)

print('Input:')
print(tiny.to_string(index=False))
print()
print('After PolynomialFeatures(degree=2):')
print(pd.DataFrame(expanded, columns=poly_demo.get_feature_names_out()).to_string(index=False))
print()
print('2 columns became', expanded.shape[1])
    Output
    Input:

    3.0    4.0
    5.0    6.0

    3.0    4.0       9.0          12.0     16.0
    5.0    6.0      25.0          30.0     36.0

    Two columns became five. On the raw housing data, the next cell gives the most dramatic result in the notebook, and it's a bad one.

    Two more results from that table stand out.

    Degree 3 is worse than degree 2. It scores 0.6294 with 83 columns, against 0.6505 with 27 for degree 2.     Interaction terms alone aren't enough here. Using only interactions, with no squared terms, scores 0.6122 with 21 columns, short of the full 0.6505. So the squared terms add real value, which suggests that some relationships here, such as income and house value, are curved.

    The biggest cost is the number of columns. Degree 2 on 6 columns gives 27. Degree 3 on the same 6 gives 83.     Wrap-upKey things to remember from this notebook#

    Scaling makes existing information usable. It doesn't add any. TotalCharges held 99.97% of the variance across Telco's three numeric columns, so four useful yes/no flags moved an unscaled KNN from just 0.7846 to 0.7848. The same flags score 0.8022 on their own, and scaling all seven columns raised AUC to 0.8272. Nothing errors when this goes wrong, which is why it's easy to miss.

    Pick a scaler based on what it's calculated from. MinMaxScaler uses only the two most extreme values, so one neighborhood averaging 1,243 people per household squeezed the middle 99% of California's neighborhoods into 0.001 to 0.004. RobustScaler, based on the median and quartiles, gave the same data a sensible spread. StandardScaler is the default. Use RobustScaler when you're keeping outliers, and MinMaxScaler only when you need a fixed range.

    Scaling changes units, not shape. All three scalers left median income's skew at exactly 1.647. To fix shape, you need a non-linear transformation.

    Know which models care. A random forest scored the same with and without scaling, since its splits only depend on order. Logistic regression also scored the same here, but converged in 11 steps instead of 54 and gave coefficients in comparable units. Regularized models like Ridge and Lasso do need scaling, because their penalty depends on units.

    Check that a transformation helped. Don't assume a stronger one is better. The reciprocal made median income worse, raising skew from 1.647 to 2.448 and reversing the order of every value. Log reached 0.226, and Box-Cox reached 0.005 by choosing its own exponent. Box-Cox needs values above zero and correctly refuses California's negative longitudes, which is why Yeo-Johnson is scikit-learn's default. Skew mostly matters for linear and distance-based models, not trees.

    Binning has a cost. Equal-width bins on tenure scored 0.8038, against 0.8087 for the untouched column, so the extra work made things worse. Notebook 1's log-and-square features reached 0.8159 with three tenure columns, while ten quantile bins needed ten columns to reach 0.8155. Use bins for explainability or real step changes, not by habit, and prefer quantile or domain-based edges over equal width.

    The order of steps matters. PolynomialFeatures on raw housing data gave an R-squared of −11.16, worse than predicting the average, because squaring a population of 35,682 gives 1.27 billion, and neither scaling nor regularization fully recovers from that (the best was 0.4507). The same expansion after a log transform reached 0.6505, the biggest gain in this notebook. Degree 3 then dropped to 0.6294, so more columns aren't always better.

    Notebook 4 moves to categorical variables. Models need numbers, categories are labels, and every way of turning one into the other makes an assumption about the data that can be right or wrong.
