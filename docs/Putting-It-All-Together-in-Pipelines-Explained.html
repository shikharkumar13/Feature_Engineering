Putting It All Together in Pipelines

Across eight notebooks this series built a toolkit: imputing what is missing, scaling what is skewed, encoding what is categorical, constructing what the file does not contain yet, selecting what earns its place. Every one of those steps carried the identical warning, and until now the only thing enforcing it was memory. This notebook turns "fit only on training data" from a habit into a property of an object, and then spends real effort measuring what memory alone was actually risking.

Datasets: Telco Customer Churn & Titanic, reused from earlier in the series Stack: pandas · scikit-learn · joblib Companion to: Notebook 9

Notebook 3 wrote the rule out in full, and it has resurfaced in some form in every notebook since: any object with a fit method learns something from data, and everything it learns must come from the training set. Followed correctly, by hand, eight times over, that rule works. It also depends entirely on nobody forgetting it, on the ninth notebook and the ninetieth. Pipeline and ColumnTransformer are the mechanism that stops the rule from being a thing to remember at all, by making the wrong order of operations awkward to write rather than easy to write by accident.

That's the honest reason to reach for them. The dishonest reason, repeated often enough to have calcified into folklore, is that skipping a pipeline will wreck your score. Section 2 measures precisely how much six different kinds of leak are actually worth, on real data, and the honest answer ranges from a millionth of a point to a catastrophic 0.27. The entire case for a pipeline is that you are never told in advance which end of that range you're standing at.

1A pipeline is one object with one fit

The entire safety property this notebook is built around fits in one sentence, and it's worth spelling out exactly what that sentence rules out

A Pipeline is a list of (name, step) pairs. Every step except the last has to implement both fit and transform; the last one only needs fit, which is why it's usually the model. Calling .fit() walks the list once, calling fit_transform on each step in turn and handing its output to the next. Calling .predict() walks the identical list calling transform only, and never calls fit again on anything.

That second sentence is the entire safety property this notebook rests on: there is no code path in which predict refits anything at all.

2How much is each kind of leak actually worth

The leakage rule has appeared once for every family of transformer in this series. Here, for the first time, all six get measured side by side, on the same two datasets, with the same model and the same seed

NotebookTransformerWhat it learns
2imputera median or a mean
3scalera mean and a standard deviation
4encodera category list, or a per-category target mean
6vectorizera vocabulary
7group aggregatea per-group statistic
8selectorwhich columns survive

Every row in that table is a fit, and every one of them can be run on the whole dataset before splitting. The usual advice stops at "so don't do that." This section measures it instead, because the sizes turn out to be wildly different, and the difference is the actually useful part. The recipe repeats for each row: the leaky version fits the transformer on all the data, then cross-validates the model on the transformed result; the honest version puts the identical transformer inside a pipeline, so every fold refits it fresh on that fold's own training portion. Same folds, same model, same seed, every time.

One part in a million, which is float arithmetic rather than a genuine finding. The reason sits directly underneath: a mean taken over all 7,032 rows and a mean taken over one training fold's 5,625 rows agree to well under one percent on every column, because a mean is a stable statistic, and dropping a random fifth of the rows barely moves it. This is the honest starting point. The leak that gets used to frighten people in tutorials is, at least here, worth nothing at all.

Two points of AUC. Notebook 4 measured this identical encoder on this identical column and reported 0.8450 leaked against 0.8250 honest; the leaked figure reproduces exactly here, and the honest one lands a ten-thousandth away. The reason lives in the category sizes: when a category holds only a handful of rows, the mean of the target over those rows is mostly made of this specific row's own label. The encoder isn't learning a property of the category anymore, it's handing the model a smoothed copy of the answer. Notebook 7 measured how that scales, the leak shrinking roughly as 1 / group_size, which is exactly why a 322-category column leaks and a 3-category column effectively doesn't. TargetEncoder avoids this by cross-fitting internally, which is not something a pipeline gives you automatically. It's something the class does on purpose, and it's worth knowing the difference between the two kinds of protection.

The same transformer, the same procedural mistake, two answers that could hardly be further apart. On 2,000 pure-noise columns and a coin-flip target, selecting first reports 74.5% accuracy for a problem whose true answer is 50%. On six real Telco columns, the identical mistake is worth exactly zero. The last two lines explain why, and it's the single most useful idea in this section: the size of a selection leak equals the instability of the selection. On the real columns, all five folds choose the identical three; choosing them in advance changes nothing because there was nothing left to exploit. On the noise columns, one survives every fold and 68 get picked by at least one, and every one of those disagreements is the selector quietly fitting itself to rows it's about to be scored against. Notebook 8 reached the identical conclusion approaching from the opposite direction, and landed on the same two counts.

Five orders of magnitude between the smallest leak and the largest, on the exact same procedural mistake.

That spread is the actual argument, and it's a different one than the argument usually handed over. If leaking a scaler cost two points of AUC, everyone would already be careful with scalers and nobody would need a pipeline to enforce it. It costs a millionth of a point instead, so being careful with scalers reads as superstition, and people generalize from that experience toward being careless with encoders and selectors, which is exactly where it costs real money. The pipeline earns its place because you cannot tell which row of that table you're standing in until after the work has already been done properly. It costs nothing at all to be right about every one of the six at once.

There's a sharper version of this same point that notebook 6 arrived at from the text-processing side: you cannot measure how much a leak is worth from inside the leaky experiment itself, because the very number you'd use to decide is the compromised one. The table above only exists because the honest column was computed too.

3ColumnTransformer, and seeing what came out of it

A plain Pipeline applies every step to every column, which is fine when every column is numeric and useless the instant it isn't

You cannot scale PaymentMethod, and you cannot one-hot encode MonthlyCharges. ColumnTransformer takes a list of (name, transformer, columns) triples, runs each one on its own slice of the table, and pastes the results back together side by side. Any one triple can itself be a small Pipeline, which is how a single column group ends up with more than one step applied to it.

The whole assembly, as one object
def count_services(frame):
    """nb7's num_services: how many of the nine services this customer actually has."""
    return frame.isin(['Yes', 'DSL', 'Fiber optic']).sum(axis=1).to_frame('num_services')

def service_feature_names(transformer, input_features):
    return ['num_services']

service_counter = FunctionTransformer(count_services, feature_names_out=service_feature_names)

def make_preprocessor():
    return ColumnTransformer(
        transformers=[
            ('num', Pipeline([('impute', SimpleImputer(strategy='median')),
                              ('scale', StandardScaler())]), NUMERIC),
            ('svc', Pipeline([('count', service_counter),
                              ('scale', StandardScaler())]), SERVICES),
            ('ord', OrdinalEncoder(categories=CONTRACT_ORDER), ORDINAL),
            ('nom', OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown='ignore', drop='if_binary',
                                  sparse_output=False), NOMINAL),
            ('hi',  TargetEncoder(random_state=SEED), ['bundle']),
        ],
        remainder='drop',
        verbose_feature_names_out=False,
    )

def make_full_pipe():
    return Pipeline([('prep', make_preprocessor()), ('model', new_lr())])

set_output(transform='pandas') is arguably the single most useful line in this entire notebook for day-to-day debugging. Without it, a ColumnTransformer hands back an unlabeled numpy array, and finding out which column just broke means counting on your fingers. With it, you get a genuine DataFrame whose names come straight from get_feature_names_out.

Two details in that output repay a closer look. verbose_feature_names_out=False is what keeps names as plain tenure rather than the more defensive default num__tenure; the verbose form exists to guarantee uniqueness, so it's safe to turn off only once you know your column groups genuinely don't collide. And drop='if_binary' is why gender produced exactly one column rather than two: for a two-category column, the second dummy is simply the first one flipped, so it carries zero additional information and would only hand a linear model a redundant coefficient to fit.

One trap is worth meeting on purpose here, since the error message alone doesn't make the cause obvious.

Pandas output meets a default one-hot encoder
sparse_prep = ColumnTransformer(
    [('nom', OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown='ignore'), NOMINAL)],   # sparse_output defaults to True
    remainder='drop').set_output(transform='pandas')

    sparse_prep.fit_transform(X)

Output
ValueError: Pandas output does not support sparse data. Set sparse_output=False to output pandas dataframes or disable Pandas output via` ohe.set_output(transform="default").

OneHotEncoder returns a sparse matrix by default, and a DataFrame simply cannot hold one, so pandas output and default one-hot settings are fundamentally incompatible. On a 322-category column, sparse is the correct choice, and it's worth keeping even at the cost of losing the DataFrame view. On seven low-cardinality columns it makes no practical difference, and sparse_output=False is worth the trade there.

The other setting that deserves a deliberate choice is remainder. It defaults to 'drop', meaning any column not explicitly listed simply gets discarded, which sounds like exactly the kind of default that should worry you and is actually the safest one available.

4Custom transformers, stateless and stateful

Sooner or later the transformation you actually need isn't in scikit-learn. The interface required to fit in is small, and one question decides everything else about how to write it

The interface itself is small: a fit that returns self, and a transform that returns the transformed data. BaseEstimator and TransformerMixin supply get_params, set_params, and fit_transform on top of that for free. Before writing a class, though, one question decides everything else: does this transformation actually learn anything?

num_services counts how many of nine columns say yes for a given row. That count depends only on the row directly in front of it: no training data involved, nothing stored anywhere, nothing that could possibly leak. A transformation like that needs no class at all, which is why the short form already appeared earlier as FunctionTransformer(count_services, feature_names_out=service_feature_names). A stateless transformer is the one genuine case where doing the work outside the pipeline is actually safe, since there's no fit step to run on the wrong rows in the first place. It still belongs inside the pipeline regardless, so the object you eventually save knows how to reproduce that column at serving time without anyone having to remember the formula separately.

Now a transformer that genuinely does learn something. Notebook 4 found that one-hot encoding all 322 bundles scored 0.8007, worse than dropping the column outright, and that grouping the rare categories together fixed it. Grouping requires knowing which categories count as rare, and that's a fact about the training rows specifically, not about the column in the abstract.

A custom grouper, fit two different ways
class RareCategoryGrouper(BaseEstimator, TransformerMixin):
    """Keep categories seen at least `min_count` times in training, send the rest to 'Other'."""

    def __init__(self, min_count=20, other_label='Other'):
        self.min_count = min_count
        self.other_label = other_label

    def fit(self, X, y=None):
        counts = pd.DataFrame(X).iloc[:, 0].value_counts()
        self.frequent_ = set(counts[counts >= self.min_count].index)
        self.n_seen_ = len(counts)
        return self

    def transform(self, X):
        column = pd.DataFrame(X).iloc[:, 0]
        return column.where(column.isin(self.frequent_), self.other_label).to_frame('bundle_grouped')

    def get_feature_names_out(self, input_features=None):
        return np.array(['bundle_grouped'])


on_everything = RareCategoryGrouper(min_count=20).fit(X[['bundle']])
X_dev, X_hold, y_dev, y_hold = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=SEED, stratify=y)
on_train_only = RareCategoryGrouper(min_count=20).fit(X_dev[['bundle']])

print('fitted on all 7,032 rows: %d categories kept, plus Other' % len(on_everything.frequent_))
print('fitted on the 5,625 training rows: %d categories kept, plus Other' % len(on_train_only.frequent_))
print()
moved = on_train_only.transform(X_hold[['bundle']])['bundle_grouped']
print('held-out rows landing in Other: %d of %d' % ((moved == 'Other').sum(), len(moved)))

fitted on the 5,625 training rows: 61 categories kept, plus Other

held-out rows landing in Other: 461 of 1407

76 categories against 61. Fitting the grouper on everything doesn't just nudge a few numbers around, it changes the actual width of the feature matrix, and it does so using rows the model is about to be scored on. This case deserves a genuinely close look, because it's the one people miss most often. RareCategoryGrouper never once touches y. It cannot leak the target, and in that specific, narrow sense it's safe. What it can still do is make your feature list itself depend on the test rows, which is precisely what happened here: fifteen extra columns exist purely because those particular rows were visible during fitting. Notebook 8 ran into the identical issue with VarianceThreshold, which is also unsupervised and also decides column membership on its own.

The distinction worth keeping

Not looking at the target is not the same thing as not looking at the data. A transformer can be entirely innocent of the leakage rule as usually stated, and still let information about your test set shape the columns your model gets to see.

Having written the class, the obvious next move is checking whether scikit-learn already does the identical job.

7What you are allowed to claim

There is now a best cross-validated score of 0.8425, and an obvious temptation to write it down as the final answer. It is not, for the exact reason notebook 8 built its own closing section around

A score that was used to choose something between competing options is no longer a clean, unbiased estimate of anything. There are three genuinely different numbers in play from here on, and keeping them apart matters.

The single idea this notebook shares with notebook 8

Selection optimism, leak size, and candidate instability are three names for the same underlying quantity. It applies without modification to choosing features, choosing hyperparameters, and choosing between entire models.

The second number is the one actually worth taking to work. Eighty-one separate fits bought two ten-thousandths of AUC, while deciding what the columns actually meant, back in notebooks 1, 4, and 7, was worth 0.0329. And even that remaining +0.0002 shouldn't be treated too seriously as a genuine gain: the five outer folds above range from 0.8358 to 0.8518, so a difference sitting in the fourth decimal place is comfortably inside the noise of the very estimate that produced it. The honest summary is that the search bought nothing measurable, and the column decisions bought three full points of AUC.

Bit-for-bit identical predictions after a round trip through disk, which is the property you actually want from a serialization step. The version line printed alongside it is not decoration.

This is the failure most likely to catch you, and it tends to catch you at the end of a long training run rather than during development, when it's cheapest to fix. Pickle stores functions and classes by reference, not by value. A lambda has no importable name anywhere, so it simply cannot be referenced, and it cannot be saved. That's the entire reason service_feature_names back in section 3 is a properly named function rather than the lambda it obviously wants to be written as.

The identical rule applies to RareCategoryGrouper. The saved file contains a pointer to the class definition, not the class itself, so whatever process eventually loads it needs that same definition importable at the same path. A custom transformer defined directly in a notebook cell will not load anywhere outside that notebook. Put custom transformers in a proper module and ship it alongside the model file. And the sklearn version line matters for exactly the same underlying reason: the file itself has no idea which version wrote it, and loading a pipeline into a different one can range anywhere from working perfectly, to failing loudly, to quietly changing behavior with no warning at all. Pin your versions next to the model file, every time.

That's the direct payoff for having been explicit earlier in the notebook. An unseen PaymentMethod becomes an all-zero block rather than a crash, because handle_unknown='ignore' was set on purpose; the prediction moves from 0.0154 to 0.0211, which is simply the model falling back on everything else it still knows about that customer. Scikit-learn raises a warning when this happens, which is exactly the right amount of noise: keep the warning, log it, and alert if the rate of occurrence ever starts climbing. An unseen bundle value receives the overall training churn rate, 0.2658, which is the only genuinely defensible guess available for a category nobody has ever seen. A NaN turning up in TotalCharges gets filled with the training median; the imputer never actually imputed anything during training, since those rows were filtered away in the very first cell, and it sits in the pipeline for exactly this future moment, since without it the identical row raises a flat Input X contains NaN error instead of a prediction. And columns can arrive in any order at all, because ColumnTransformer selects by name rather than position; an extra column gets quietly ignored, because remainder='drop'; a missing column fails immediately, and names exactly which one is missing. That whole last group is a schema check you got for free, purely by having listed your columns explicitly in the first place.

Key things to remember from this notebook

A pipeline does not make your model better. Assembling one made this one better, going from 0.8087 on three numeric columns to 0.8416 with everything handled properly, but every one of those gains was decided in an earlier notebook by thinking carefully about what a column actually means. The pipeline is what stops that work from getting subtly undone by accident.

The leakage rule is worth wildly different amounts depending on exactly what's leaking. Fitting a scaler on all the data cost 0.000001 here; fitting an imputer cost 0.000134. Both are nothing, because both learn aggregate statistics that a fifth of the rows barely move. Target encoding a 322-category column cost 0.020, and selecting 20 features out of 2,000 noise columns cost 0.27. You never get to know in advance which case you're standing in, and a pipeline covers all of them at no extra charge.

Not looking at the target is not the same as not looking at the data. RareCategoryGrouper never touches y at all, and still kept 76 categories when fit on everything against 61 when fit on training rows alone, changing the width of the feature matrix using rows it was about to be scored on.

ColumnTransformer wants explicit column lists, and remainder='drop' should stay the default. That single setting kept customerID out of the model entirely, and it turns the transformer into a schema that fails loudly the moment the input's shape changes.

Turn on set_output(transform='pandas') while building. Debugging an unlabeled numpy array of eighteen columns wastes an afternoon. Remember it's incompatible with sparse one-hot output, which is a real constraint on any wide categorical column.

Ask whether a transformer learns anything before writing a class for it. Stateless work like counting services fits inside a FunctionTransformer. Stateful work needs a proper fit to store what it learned. Check whether scikit-learn already has it first: the hand-rolled rare-category grouper scored 0.8416 against 0.8415 for OneHotEncoder(min_frequency=20), one argument instead of a class to maintain indefinitely.

A feature is worth whatever it adds to the features already present, not a fixed amount. bundle was worth 0.0163 to notebook 4 on a bare numeric baseline, and only 0.0017 here, once Contract, PaymentMethod, InternetService, and num_services were already in the model. Three different ways of encoding it finished within 0.0001 of each other.

Preprocessing settings become hyperparameters once they live in the pipeline, and searching them was nearly pointless here. All 81 combinations spanned 0.0072 end to end, most of that came from the model's own regularization strength rather than preprocessing, and the whole search improved the honest estimate by 0.0002. Search tunes the last percent. Deciding that Contract is ordinal was worth considerably more than the entire grid combined.

A score you used to choose something is no longer a score you're entitled to quote. Here, quoting the best of 81 was inflated by only 0.0001, because the 81 candidates all agreed closely with each other. Notebook 8 got 0.27 of inflation from that identical act, because its candidates did not agree at all. Selection optimism, leak size, and candidate instability are the same quantity wearing three different names.

Be careful about reading a single hold-out as the truth. Across eight random 20% splits, the gap between cross-validated and held-out AUC had a mean of +0.0002, a standard deviation of 0.0113, and a range from −0.0146 to +0.0185. The specific gap observed once was noise, not optimism, and it was larger than every effect the entire grid search managed to find.

Save with joblib, and remember that pickle stores code by reference. Lambdas cannot be saved at all. Custom classes must stay importable at the identical path in whatever process eventually loads them. Nothing in the saved file enforces which sklearn version wrote it.

The one sentence: a pipeline turns "remember to fit only on training data" from a habit you have to maintain into a property of an object you can simply hand to someone else.

Notebook 10 closes the series by going after the failures that survive all of this. A pipeline makes leakage through preprocessing structurally impossible, and it does nothing whatsoever about a column that should never have been collected in the first place, a feature that could not actually be computed at prediction time in production, or a model that got tuned until the validation set quietly stopped being a validation set at all.
