Feature Engineering for Text Data

Every other notebook in this series started with a table someone had already built: rows, columns, a place to begin arguing about which columns mattered. A raw SMS message has none of that. There is no column for how urgent it sounds. There is no column at all, just a string of characters, and every number this notebook produces from it is one this notebook has to invent and then justify on its own.

Datasets: SMS Spam Collection (5,571 messages) & Sentiment Labelled Sentences (3,000 reviews) Stack: pandas · scikit-learn Companion to: Notebook 6

Turning strings into a numeric matrix, and then arguing about which numbers were worth inventing: that is the whole shape of this notebook. The spine of it is the SMS Spam Collection, 5,571 real text messages labelled ham or spam, chosen because the two classes differ in ways that both word choice and writing style can pick up independently, which lets the notebook compare those two ideas honestly rather than assuming one wins. A second, smaller dataset of 3,000 labelled review sentences from Amazon, IMDB and Yelp joins in later, for one specific question spam detection cannot answer on its own.

Notebook 2 made this case for tables. Text logs are where it bites hardest, because real conversations repeat themselves constantly

Notebook 2 established that duplicate rows inflate a score, because the identical row can land in both a training fold and the test fold that's supposed to be evaluating it honestly. Text is where this problem is worst, because real message logs are full of genuine repeats: template spam blasted to thousands of numbers, and people who really do send "Ok..." or "Sorry, I'll call later" over and over.

2The vocabulary idea, and what it throws away

Bag of words is the classic starting point, and it's worth building one by hand once so the mechanism is never a mystery

The classic way to turn text into numbers: collect every distinct word across all documents into a vocabulary, give each document a row and each vocabulary word a column, and fill each cell with how many times that word appears in that document. The result is a document-term matrix.

The standard repair is n-grams: treat adjacent runs of words as tokens in their own right, so a bigram model gets a column for not good in addition to separate columns for not and good.

Bigrams don't give a model any understanding of grammar. They give it a specific, finite set of short phrases that happen to carry meaning the individual words lost on their own, which is genuinely useful and just as genuinely limited. It also isn't free: section 4 measures exactly what it costs in vocabulary size.

Storing it densely would cost 360 MB to hold 68,240 actually useful numbers; the sparse representation scikit-learn returns by default costs 0.84 MB, about 430 times smaller. This is exactly why every vectorizer in scikit-learn returns a scipy.sparse matrix rather than a plain numpy array, and it has a direct practical consequence worth internalizing before it costs you a session: calling .toarray() on a real document-term matrix is how people run out of memory. It was fine on the four-by-fourteen toy example above. It is not fine at 5,169 by 8,672, and it gets dramatically worse on a corpus of millions.

Why is it so empty in the first place? Because word frequencies are wildly unequal.

That imbalance is the entire justification for a vectorizer's two most useful arguments: min_df drops terms appearing in fewer than N documents, clearing out the long tail of one-offs, and max_df drops terms appearing in more than a given fraction of documents, clearing out words too common to discriminate anything.

A trap worth reading twice

Both arguments count documents, not raw occurrences, and the two numbers genuinely disagree. A word used five times inside a single message has a term frequency of 5 but a document frequency of only 1, so min_df=2 removes it entirely. Reasoning about min_df using total occurrence counts would predict 3,914 surviving terms at a cutoff of 2; the real answer, using document frequency as the vectorizer actually does, is 3,802. The gap is exactly the words that repeat inside a single message without ever spreading to a second one.

5TF-IDF, and how much the weighting actually buys

Raw counts have an obvious flaw: the word "the" out-counts almost anything meaningful in nearly every document, contributing volume without contributing any distinction

TF-IDF rescales each raw count by how rare that word is across the whole corpus. TF, term frequency, is how often a word appears in this specific document. IDF, inverse document frequency, measures how rare it is across every document. The stored value is their product, and each row then gets normalized to unit length.

Textbooks usually write IDF as log(N / df), which goes all the way to zero for a word appearing in every single document. Scikit-learn doesn't use that formula by default. With smooth_idf=True, its default, it computes

idf = ln((1 + N) / (1 + df)) + 1

The trailing +1 matters directly for reading the output: a word appearing in every document bottoms out at exactly 1.0, never 0, so no term is ever multiplied away to nothing. The smoothing inside the logarithm behaves as if one extra document existed containing every word, which keeps the denominator from ever reaching zero for a term that turns out to be universal.

7The leakage rule, in its text-shaped form

The rule from notebook 3, restated once more: anything with a fit method learns from data, and everything it learns has to come from training rows only

CountVectorizer and TfidfVectorizer both have a fit method, and both learn two things from whatever data they're shown: the vocabulary itself, meaning which words get a column at all, and the IDF weights, which depend directly on document frequencies measured across the corpus. Calling vectorizer.fit_transform(all_text) and only afterward splitting into train and test is leakage, full stop. The test documents helped decide which words exist as columns and how heavily each one gets weighted. This is easily the most common leak in real text pipelines, precisely because writing it that way feels completely natural.

The wrong way against the right way, measured directly

8Hand-crafted features, which turn out to beat the vocabulary

Everything so far has asked which words appear. Go back to section 1's messages and notice how much signal lives somewhere else entirely: in how a message is written

Spam has to include some way to actually act on it: a phone number, a short code, a price, a URL. Those leave numeric fingerprints that have nothing to do with any particular word choice, and every one of them survives a spammer simply rewording their pitch.

Nine style features, built and inspected before any modeling
class StyleFeatures(BaseEstimator, TransformerMixin):
    # Turns raw strings into a dense array of writing-style statistics.
    # Stateless: fit does nothing, so there is nothing here that can leak.
    names = ['n_chars', 'n_words', 'avg_word_len', 'digit_ratio', 'upper_ratio',
             'n_exclam', 'n_currency', 'has_url', 'n_longnum']

    def fit(self, X, y=None):
        return self

    def transform(self, X):
        rows = []
        for text in X:
            words = text.split()
            length = max(len(text), 1)
            rows.append([
                len(text),
                len(words),
                np.mean([len(w) for w in words]) if words else 0.0,
                sum(c.isdigit() for c in text) / length,
                sum(c.isupper() for c in text) / length,
                text.count('!'),
                sum(text.count(c) for c in '£$€'),
                float(bool(re.search(r'http|www\.', text, re.I))),
                len(re.findall(r'\d{5,}', text)),          # phone numbers, short codes
            ])
        return np.asarray(rows, dtype=float)

    def get_feature_names_out(self, input_features=None):
        return np.array(self.names)

    for metric in ['roc_auc', 'f1', 'recall', 'precision']:
        row[metric] = cross_val_score(estimator, X_text, y, cv=CV5,
                                      scoring=metric, n_jobs=1).mean()
    return row

9Combining sparse and dense features

TF-IDF and style features are describing genuinely different things about the same message, so the obvious next move is to use both at once

The mechanical obstacle is that TF-IDF returns a sparse matrix and StyleFeatures returns a dense array. FeatureUnion exists precisely for this: it runs both branches on the same input and concatenates the results while keeping the combined output sparse, all inside one estimator, so cross-validation still correctly refits each branch on every fold.

Style alone, words alone, and both together
word_tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(min_df=2)
char_tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(analyzer='char_wb', ngram_range=(2, 4), min_df=2)

def combo(*branches):
    return Pipeline([
        ('features', FeatureUnion(list(branches))),
        ('clf', LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000)),
    ])

10When words are the wrong unit

Two more representations, both addressing a weakness this notebook has already run straight into

Character n-grams slide a window over characters instead of words. That sounds unusual until you remember what SMS text actually looks like: txt, 2morrow, FREEEE, w1n. A word-level vocabulary treats every spelling variant as its own separate, unseen token. A character-level model sees the substrings those variants still share.

Word n-grams against character n-grams, on identical folds

Hashing attacks a different problem entirely. Every vectorizer used so far stores a Python dictionary mapping terms to column indices, and section 3 already showed that dictionary never stops growing as more data arrives. HashingVectorizer replaces the lookup with a hash function outright: the column for a term is simply hash(term) % n_features. No dictionary anywhere, no fit step, and a fixed output width forever regardless of how much text eventually flows through it.

The collision column is the genuine surprise here. At 1,024 features, 88% of the real vocabulary shares a column with some other term, and the model still scores 0.9823, only about 0.007 below full TF-IDF. Collisions are far less damaging than they sound at first, because the terms that collide are overwhelmingly the rare ones from section 3's long tail, and a collision between two words that each show up exactly once is close to harmless by construction. What that half a point buys: no vocabulary sitting in memory and none to ship alongside the model, no fit step at all so it's stateless and trivially parallelizable, unseen words landing in some column rather than being silently dropped, and a fixed width forever, so a stream of new data can never grow the feature space out from under you. What it costs: the collisions measured directly above, which are cheap here but never entirely free, and interpretability, since a column can no longer be mapped back to a specific word, which means the coefficient inspection from section 5 simply isn't available anymore. This is the identical trade as the hashing encoder from notebook 4, and it's fundamentally a deployment decision more than a modeling one.

Key things to remember from this notebook

Text becomes a matrix, and that matrix is almost entirely empty. 5,169 SMS messages produced an 8,672-word vocabulary that was 99.85% zeros. Dense storage would have cost 360 MB against 0.84 MB sparse. Keep it sparse, and be careful with .toarray() on anything real.

Half the vocabulary is noise. 55% of terms occurred exactly once in the whole corpus. Setting min_df=3 cut the vocabulary by roughly 70% and the AUC went slightly up. min_df and max_df count documents, not occurrences, and mixing the two up gives a genuinely wrong prediction of what survives.

Bag of words discards word order, and n-grams only partly repair it. Two sentences with opposite meanings produced identical unigram vectors; adding bigrams separated them through the single feature not good. On spam, though, bigrams bought a tiny AUC gain for over three times the vocabulary and actually lowered F1. Whether a technique helps is a property of the task, never a property of the technique alone.

TF-IDF is a good default and a modest effect. It beat raw counts by +0.0019 here. It matters most when a model can't already learn to downweight common words on its own, such as a distance-based method with no built-in way to rescale features.

Do not remove stopwords on autopilot. Scikit-learn's list contains not, no, never and without. On review sentiment, blanket removal cost 0.0067 accuracy, while removing stopwords and putting twelve negation words back scored 0.8391, beating both alternatives. On spam it changed nothing at all. Same step, opposite verdicts, depending entirely on the task.

Vectorizers have a fit method, so the leakage rule applies to them too. Fitting on all data before splitting put 639 terms into the vocabulary purely because test documents got a vote, 17% of it at a 25% test split, rising to 79% at a 90% split. It moved AUC by only 0.0004 at the smaller split, but a leak's true size can never be measured from inside the leaky experiment itself, which is exactly why the rule has to be procedural. Put the vectorizer in a Pipeline and the question stops needing to be asked case by case.

Look at how text is written, not only which words it uses. Nine style features beat a 3,796-term vocabulary on F1, and a random forest on those same nine columns reached 0.9373. Spam structurally has to include a phone number or a price to be worth sending, and that constraint survives any amount of rewording.

The metric decides which features look worthwhile. Adding style features to word TF-IDF moved AUC by about +0.003 and recall by nearly +0.08. Watching only AUC would have said not to bother.

Deduplicate before modeling anything. Repeated messages, concentrated in spam at more than double ham's rate, inflated AUC by a real if modest amount here, on a model already near its ceiling. The fix is one line, so there's little reason to gamble on whether a weaker model or a messier dataset would have made the cost larger.

Words are not the only unit worth trying. Character n-grams gave the best text-only result in the notebook, because SMS spelling is genuinely chaotic. Hashing gives up a little accuracy and all interpretability in exchange for never storing a vocabulary, and it proved remarkably robust to collisions, since the terms that collide are overwhelmingly the rare ones that carried little weight regardless.

Check the shape, not just the class mean. A mean of roughly 1.0 for a digit-run feature reads like "every spam message has exactly one phone number." In fact close to a quarter of spam had none at all. A mean tells you the center of a distribution and nothing whatsoever about its spread.
