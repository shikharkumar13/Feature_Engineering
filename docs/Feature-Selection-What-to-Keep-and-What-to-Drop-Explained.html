Feature Selection: What to Keep and What to Drop

Notebook 7 spent its length adding columns and closed by warning that automated tools will add hundreds more without being asked. This one takes the other side. Which features are actually helping turns out to be one of the easiest questions in a project to get confidently, invisibly wrong, and by the end of this notebook a model reports 74.5% accuracy on a target that is provably, mathematically a coin flip.

Dataset: a synthetic 1,000-row file with a known, graded answer (5 real signals, 5 disguised copies, 5 near-constants, 15 pure noise) Stack: pandas · scikit-learn Companion to: Notebook 8

Choosing features using the whole dataset and then cross-validating the result is leakage. It's the identical rule that governed imputers, scalers, encoders, target encoders, and group aggregates in every earlier notebook, and here it produces the most dramatic failure in the entire series: a model that reports a respectable-looking score on data that is, by construction, unlearnable. Everything before that point in this notebook is about a quieter problem, one with no leakage involved at all: several genuinely popular methods for deciding what a column is worth, each with a specific, demonstrable blind spot.

1A dataset where we know the answer

Feature selection is the one topic in this series that needs ground truth to grade a method, since on real data you can measure whether a selection improved your score but never whether it kept the right columns

The file behind this notebook has 1,000 rows, a balanced binary target, and 30 features arranged in four groups the names give away: info_0 through info_4, genuinely related to the target; corr_0 through corr_4, each one its matching info_ column plus added noise; const_0 through const_4, constant or nearly so; and fifteen noise_ columns carrying nothing at all. A good method should keep the five real signals, notice the five disguised copies are redundant, and discard everything else. Every method in this notebook gets graded against that exact standard.

2Does dropping features actually help?

Feature selection is often justified with a general appeal to overfitting. Before learning any method, it's worth checking that claim on a case where the useless columns are known in advance

Four feature sets, the same model, the same folds
def auc(cols, frame=None):
    frame = X_all if frame is None else frame
    est = Pipeline([('scale', StandardScaler()),
                    ('clf', LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000))])
    return cross_val_score(est, frame[cols], y, cv=CV5,
                           scoring='roc_auc', n_jobs=1).mean()

Three things worth reading directly off that table. The fifteen noise columns alone score 0.5038, which is chance: the data really is exactly what it claims to be. Five features beat thirty, 0.9999 against 0.9959, which means dropping twenty-five columns didn't merely cost nothing, it made the model measurably better. And the gain itself, +0.0040, is small in absolute terms, because logistic regression with L2 regularization is already fairly robust to irrelevant inputs; on a model with less regularization, fewer rows, or a harder problem, that gap widens considerably.

So selection is worth doing here, and there's a specific, checkable target to aim at: recover those five columns. It's worth holding onto the size of the prize, though. Most of the real value of feature selection in an actual project is not the fourth decimal place of AUC. It's a model that trains faster, ships smaller, has fewer things to monitor, fewer upstream dependencies that can break it, and an explanation a colleague can actually follow.

3The free wins, and why the usual tool misses most of them

The cheapest possible filter needs no target and no model at all: a column that barely varies cannot separate anything. The standard tool for this turns out to be mostly wrong on this dataset

Only three of the five near-constant columns get caught. const_3 is the instructive survivor: 97.1% of its rows hold the identical value, and its variance is still 0.70, higher than several columns worth actually keeping. The reason is that variance measures spread in the column's own units, not concentration. const_3 sits at 5.0 for 971 rows and somewhere far away for the other 29, and squared distance from the mean does the rest: a column can be 97% one value and still carry a respectable variance if the remaining 3% sits far enough out. That points directly at the actual fix. If what you mean is "nearly always the same value," the honest move is to measure that directly rather than reaching for variance as a proxy for it.

Measuring concentration directly, instead of spread
def dominant_share(frame):
    # Share of rows taken by each column's single most common value.
    return frame.apply(lambda s: s.value_counts(normalize=True).iloc[0])


shares = dominant_share(X_all)
quasi_constant = shares[shares > 0.95].sort_values(ascending=False)

print('columns where one value covers more than 95% of rows:')
print(quasi_constant.round(4).to_string())
print()
print(f'that finds {len(quasi_constant)} of the {len(GROUPS["const"])} const_ columns, '
      f'and nothing else')

All five, with no false positives, from two lines of code and no threshold to tune against an arbitrary variance scale. There's a second problem with VarianceThreshold that matters more than the first, precisely because it fails silently: variance depends on units. A distance in millimeters has a variance a million times larger than the identical distance in kilometers, and whatever threshold you picked has no way to know which one it's looking at.

Output

  dropped: ['info_0', 'const_0', 'const_1', 'const_2']

info_0 is the single most predictive column in the dataset (|corr| with target = 0.5529)

info_0 is the single strongest predictor in the entire file, and dividing it by 1,000 is enough to have it silently discarded. Nothing about its relationship to the target changed in that operation; only the number sitting in a units column did. The practical guidance ends up narrower than the usual advice: use VarianceThreshold(0) to remove genuinely constant columns, where units genuinely cannot matter, since a column that never varies has variance exactly zero regardless of scale. For quasi-constant columns, count the dominant value's share instead. And if a variance threshold is unavoidable, scale first, and go in knowing you're then thresholding on a quantity with no interpretable unit of its own.

4Redundancy, and a heuristic that keeps the wrong column

Two columns carrying the same information are not twice as useful. For a linear model they actively hurt, and the standard fix for that has a flaw worth seeing directly

Multicollinearity makes a linear model's coefficients unstable and hard to interpret, since the fit can trade weight freely between two columns that say almost the same thing. The standard recipe: find pairs above some correlation threshold, and drop the weaker of each, where "weaker" means less correlated with the target. That tie-break sounds obviously correct.

The standard heuristic, run on this dataset
corr_matrix = X.corr().abs()
target_corr = X.apply(lambda c: abs(np.corrcoef(c, y)[0, 1]))

to_drop = set()
cols = corr_matrix.columns.tolist()
for i in range(len(cols)):
    for j in range(i + 1, len(cols)):
        if corr_matrix.iloc[i, j] > 0.9:
            weaker = cols[i] if target_corr[cols[i]] < target_corr[cols[j]] else cols[j]
            to_drop.add(weaker)

print(f'pairs above |r| = 0.9, dropping the weaker of each: {sorted(to_drop)}')
print()
print('the two decisions worth looking at:')
for i in [3, 4]:
    print(f'  info_{i}: |corr with target| = {target_corr[f"info_{i}"]:.4f}')
    print(f'  corr_{i}: |corr with target| = {target_corr[f"corr_{i}"]:.4f}   <- kept')
Output
pairs above |r| = 0.9, dropping the weaker of each: ['corr_0', 'corr_1', 'corr_2', 'info_3', 'info_4']

the two decisions worth looking at:
  info_3: |corr with target| = 0.2001
  corr_3: |corr with target| = 0.2052   <- kept
  info_4: |corr with target| = 0.1414
  corr_4: |corr with target| = 0.1415   <- kept

The heuristic drops info_3 and info_4, the genuine features, and keeps corr_3 and corr_4, which are those same features with noise stirred in. This isn't a bug in the implementation. corr_3 genuinely does correlate marginally more with the target in this specific sample, 0.2052 against 0.2001, and corr_4 wins by even less, 0.1415 against 0.1414. Those margins are sampling noise, not signal, and the rule has no mechanism whatsoever for telling a real edge from a coin flip. In the info_4 case, the entire decision turns on the fourth decimal place.

Does the mistake actually cost anything, on the score?
kept = [c for c in X.columns if c not in to_drop]
print(f'before removal, {len(X.columns)} columns: {auc(list(X.columns), X):.4f}')
print(f'after removal,  {len(kept)} columns: {auc(kept, X):.4f}')
Output
before removal, 28 columns: 0.9959
after removal,  23 columns: 0.9959

Identical to four decimal places, which makes complete sense once you see why: each column was swapped for a near-copy of itself, so the model loses almost nothing it can measure. It matters for everything else downstream. The surviving model now runs on corr_3 and corr_4, columns that in a real project would typically be some derived, downstream field rather than the actual measurement driving the outcome. Every explanation offered afterward, every monitoring alert configured, every conversation about why the model behaves the way it does now points at the wrong column.

Two habits help here. When a correlated pair is a genuine near-tie, break it on which column you'd rather depend on: the one that's cheaper to compute, more stable upstream, or easier to explain to the next person who has to maintain it. And absent a strong reason otherwise, prefer keeping the more fundamental measurement over anything derived from it.

5Univariate filters, and why the top five are not the best five

The next family scores each feature against the target entirely on its own. Fast, model-free, and built with one specific structural blind spot

f_classif runs a one-way ANOVA per column, which for a binary target is essentially asking how far apart the two class means sit relative to the spread within each class.

Three signals, not five, because two of the five slots went to near-duplicates of columns already picked. The result, 0.9674, sits well behind the 0.9999 available from the correct five, and behind even the top ten. This is the defining limitation of univariate selection: it scores every feature in isolation, so it has no way to know that corr_0 says almost exactly what info_0 already said. Each one individually looks excellent, and the pair together is nearly worthless as a duo.

Two consequences follow. Univariate filters pair badly with redundant feature sets, which describes most feature sets that come out of a notebook like the previous one in this series. And if you do reach for one, either remove redundancy first, the way section 4 attempted and imperfectly managed, or use it as a coarse, cheap pre-filter ahead of something multivariate rather than as the final word on which columns survive.

6Mutual information, and its blind spot

Pearson correlation and the F test both look for one specific shape of relationship. Mutual information asks a more general question, and generality is never free

A feature could be strongly related to the target in a way neither correlation nor an F test can see: a U-shape, say, where both very high and very low values point to the same class. Mutual information asks a broader question instead: how much does knowing this feature reduce uncertainty about the target? It assumes nothing about the shape of the relationship, and a true value of zero means genuine statistical independence.

info_4 scores exactly 0.0000, and a noise column scores 0.0222. Mutual information ranks a genuinely informative feature below pure noise. info_4 is real but weak, correlating only 0.1414 with the target, and scikit-learn estimates mutual information with a nearest-neighbor method that carries its own estimation variance. On 1,000 rows, a relationship this faint sits inside that estimator's noise floor and clips at zero. noise_11 picks up a small positive score for the identical reason, estimation noise, just pointed in the other direction.

The honest summary: mutual information catches relationships correlation and F tests genuinely miss, which is a real advantage on non-linear problems. It's an estimate, not a measurement, and its error is largest exactly where the stakes are highest, on weak signals. And a zero from it does not mean independence; it means the estimator couldn't find anything, which is a different and weaker claim. The F test, on the same weak column, ranked info_4 at 20.4 against the best noise column's 3.3. When a relationship is roughly linear, the simpler tool turns out to be the more reliable one.

One more practical detail matters more than it looks. mutual_info_classif adds a small amount of noise internally to break ties between identical values, and its random_state defaults to None. Left unpinned, it returns a different answer on every single call.

The same code, run repeatedly, with and without a pinned seed
def mi_pipeline(scorer):
    return Pipeline([('scale', StandardScaler()),
                     ('select', SelectKBest(scorer, k=10)),
                     ('clf', LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000))])


unpinned = [cross_val_score(mi_pipeline(mutual_info_classif), X, y, cv=CV5,
                            scoring='roc_auc', n_jobs=1).mean() for _ in range(5)]

                          scoring='roc_auc', n_jobs=1).mean() for _ in range(3)]

The unpinned version moves in the fourth decimal place on every run, enough to make any number quoted from it unreproducible by the next person who runs the same cell. Every use of mutual information in this notebook goes through a wrapper that pins the seed. This generalizes past mutual information specifically: any scorer, selector, or model carrying a random_state left at its default will quietly make results unrepeatable, and feature selection is a place where that bites twice over, since both the score and the actual list of columns kept can shift between runs.

7Tree importance, and why the default one is misleading

A random forest reports a feature importance for free. It is also the most widely used importance in practice, and it carries a well-documented bias this dataset shows plainly

Every split on a feature reduces impurity somewhat, and summing those reductions across every tree in the forest produces a score per column, normalized to sum to 1 across the whole model.

Impurity importance against permutation importance, side by side

Impurity importance hands 18.7% of its total to columns that are pure noise. Permutation importance gives those same fifteen columns 0.0007 between them, which is 0.22% of its own total of 0.3203. The two totals aren't directly comparable, and it's worth being precise about why: impurity importances are normalized to sum to exactly 1, while permutation importances are raw score drops that sum to whatever they happen to sum to. Compare the shares, never the raw numbers against each other.

The cause is structural rather than incidental. A continuous noise column offers a tree many distinct candidate split points, and with enough of them, some split will reduce impurity on the training rows purely by chance. That chance reduction gets recorded as importance regardless of whether it generalizes to a single row outside the training set. Impurity importance is computed entirely on training data, so it rewards the raw capacity to overfit. Permutation importance asks a genuinely different question: shuffle this one column and see how much the score actually gets worse. A column the model was only ever using to memorize training rows costs nothing when shuffled, so it correctly scores at or near zero.

The correlated columns expose a second, separate problem with impurity importance.

8L1, where selection happens during training

Every method so far decides before or after training. An L1 penalty decides during it, and selection and fitting become a single step

A penalty proportional to the sum of absolute coefficients pushes weights toward zero, and unlike an L2 penalty, it can push them all the way to exactly zero. A feature with a zero coefficient is, for every practical purpose, not in the model at all. In scikit-learn's logistic regression, C is the inverse of regularization strength, so a small C means a heavy penalty and few survivors.

Sweeping C, and watching who survives at each value

The path is easy to read, and it says something genuinely useful at both ends. At C = 0.03, the selection is exactly right: all five info_ columns, one corr_, zero noise. Tighten to C = 0.01 and it starts discarding real signal, losing info_4, the weakest of the five. Loosen to C = 0.3 and beyond, and noise floods back in, five columns growing to eleven and then more.

Notice also that L1 keeps only one column from each correlated pair. Faced with two nearly identical columns, it puts its weight on one and drives the other to exactly zero, because paying the penalty twice for the same information is simply wasteful under its objective. That's the opposite of impurity importance's behavior, which split credit between correlated columns instead of choosing one, and it's usually the behavior you actually want from a selector.

The catch is that which member of the pair survives is arbitrary and unstable. Resample the data and L1 may well swap info_3 for corr_3 on the next run. If stable selection across genuinely correlated groups matters for your use case, that instability is exactly what elastic net exists to address. And C is doing double duty here, controlling both raw regularization strength and, as a side effect, how many features survive; tuning it against cross-validated score is the honest way to set it, producing a real trade-off rather than a threshold invented on the spot.

9Recursive elimination, and the payoff for thinking jointly

Section 5 left a question hanging. What does a method that judges features together, rather than one at a time, do with the exact same redundancy problem?

Recursive feature elimination fits the model, drops the single least useful column by whatever criterion the model provides, and repeats on what remains. Because the model gets refit after every removal, the importance of each surviving column is judged in the presence of everything still left, not in isolation. Once info_0 is safely in the surviving set, corr_0 has nothing left to contribute and gets eliminated on a later pass.

On this dataset, RFE recovers info_0 through info_4 exactly, all five genuine signals, and scores 0.9999, matching the ground truth precisely. That's the argument for wrapper methods stated as a single table: univariate filtering spent the same budget of five columns and reached only 0.9674, because it had no way to account for what it had already picked. It's worth being precise about how much to generalize from this, though, since it's an empirical result on one dataset rather than a guarantee baked into the algorithm. RFE's mechanism, judging each remaining column in the presence of the others, makes recovering the truly independent signals systematically likely once a genuine one is already in the set. It doesn't make it certain on every dataset with every correlation structure; the size of the advantage depends on exactly how redundant the columns are and how the elimination happens to unfold. What's true without qualification is the mechanism itself: RFE cannot fall into section 5's specific trap, because judging jointly is the entire point of refitting after each removal.

The cost is compute. RFE fits the underlying model once per elimination step, so a naive run against 1,000 candidate features means 1,000 separate fits, and RFECV multiplies that by the number of cross-validation folds on top. The usual compromise filters cheaply down to a manageable candidate set first, then runs RFE on what remains; setting step to a fraction, 0.1 to drop 10% of the remaining columns at a time rather than one by one, also helps considerably.

One thing RFE does not do on its own is tell you how many features to keep. Here it was told to keep five because the answer was already known in advance. RFECV picks that number by cross-validation instead, which is the honest approach when you genuinely don't know, and the next section explains in detail why that step has to be done with real care.

10The mistake that makes all of this worthless

Most methods in this notebook look directly at the target. That single distinction decides which of them can leak

Section 3's two filters, VarianceThreshold and the dominant-value share, are computed from the feature columns alone and never once touch y. A selector that never sees the target cannot leak the target, so those two are genuinely safe to run before splitting. Everything from section 4 onward, including the correlation tie-break, learns from the labels, and the rule already established in notebook 3 applies without modification: anything that learns from data must learn only from training rows.

A scope worth being precise about

Unsupervised filters are safe from this specific leak, not from every concern that exists. Computing them on the full dataset still means your final feature list depends on rows you technically haven't split off yet, which matters once new data arrives with a different distribution. It simply won't inflate your cross-validation score the way a supervised selector's leak does.

Select features using the whole dataset, then cross-validate the chosen subset, and the test folds have quietly helped choose their own features. The cross-validation score that comes out the other end is no longer an estimate of anything real. The clearest way to see exactly how bad this gets is to run the identical procedure on data engineered to contain no signal whatsoever.

200 rows, 2,000 pure-noise columns, a coin-flip target

The mechanism is worth stating with precision, because it isn't obvious on first pass. With 2,000 columns and only 200 rows, some column will correlate with the target by pure luck, and here the best of them reaches 0.2343. Ranking all 2,000 against the target picks out exactly those lucky columns. Their luck was measured across all 200 rows, so it persists unchanged into every single test fold. The model isn't learning a pattern at all; it's re-reading an accident it already saw during selection.

That mechanism also predicts precisely how the illusion scales: the more columns searched, the luckier the very best one gets to look.

def selections_per_fold(frame, labels, k, splitter):
    picks = []
    for train_idx, _ in splitter.split(frame, labels):

    return picks

Selection leakage is worth roughly as much as the instability of the selection itself. That's a genuine diagnostic, not merely a superstition to follow on faith: if refitting the selector on each fold returns the same columns every time, the selection is stable and the leak is small regardless of the procedural sin committed. If the selected set churns noticeably between folds, the dangerous regime has been entered, and the reported score is closer to fiction than estimate.

This also supplies the right answer to the reasonable objection that doing selection properly is expensive. Sometimes it genuinely is, and the fold-agreement check above is exactly the tool that tells you whether skipping the correct procedure is a risk worth taking. But notice the trap hiding inside that shortcut: the harmlessness of the leak on the real dataset could only be established by first doing the selection properly and comparing. There's no way to reach that same conclusion from inside the leaky version alone, which is the identical point notebook 6 made about vectorizer vocabularies. The rule stands regardless of how it turns out on any one dataset: put the selector inside the pipeline. It costs one line.

chosen by at least one : 9 columns

Five methods, and the pattern here is more interesting than plain disagreement. Three of them, univariate F, forest impurity, and permutation, land on exactly the same six columns: the three strongest signals plus all three of their noisy copies. They disagree sharply on how to rank those six, as section 7 already demonstrated in detail, but the set itself is identical across all three. Mutual information swaps one copy out for info_3 instead. Only L1 recovers all five genuinely informative columns, because it's the only one of the five methods actually penalized for keeping a redundant column around.

So there are three distinct answers among five separate methods, and only info_0, info_1, and info_2, simply the three strongest signals in the file, are unanimous across every one of them.

None of the five methods is broken. They're answering genuinely different questions, and a column can be important under one definition of "important" while being worthless under another; the correlated pairs in this dataset are simply the clearest possible case of that gap showing up in practice.

What this means in practice: agreement across methods is meaningful on its own. A column every method keeps is safe to keep, and one every method discards is safe to drop without further ceremony. Disagreement is a prompt rather than something to resolve by picking a favorite method and trusting it blindly; it usually means the column in question is redundant with something else already selected, genuinely weak, or non-linear in a way one particular method simply cannot see. The only real arbiter, in the end, is cross-validated score on the metric that actually matters for the problem at hand, with the selector sitting properly inside the pipeline throughout. Everything else covered in this notebook is a heuristic for generating candidates worth that final test, not a substitute for it.

Key things to remember from this notebook

Selection can improve a model, not just shrink it. Five columns beat thirty here, 0.9999 against 0.9959. The bigger prizes in a real project are usually the ones that never show up in the metric at all: faster training, smaller artifacts, fewer upstream dependencies, an explanation that fits in one sentence.

VarianceThreshold is a weak tool for quasi-constant columns. It caught three of five here, missing const_3, which holds one value in 97.1% of rows and still carries a variance of 0.70. Counting the share taken by the most common value instead found all five and nothing else.

Variance depends on units. Dividing info_0, the most predictive column in the file, by 1,000 was enough for VarianceThreshold(0.01) to discard it silently. Use a zero threshold for genuinely constant columns, and scale first before thresholding on anything else.

"Keep whichever correlates more with the target" is a coin flip when the margin is thin. It dropped the real info_3 and info_4 for noisy copies that happened to win by 0.005 of correlation. The score didn't move, but every later explanation now points at the wrong column.

Univariate filters cannot see redundancy. The top five by F score held only three distinct signals and scored 0.9674, because two slots went to near-duplicates. RFE, which refits after each removal, recovered all five originals and reached 0.9999.

Mutual information is an estimate, and a zero is not proof of independence. It scored info_4, a genuinely informative column, at exactly 0.0000, below a noise column at 0.0222. It earns its place on non-linear relationships; on roughly linear ones, the F test was both simpler and more reliable.

Default tree importance rewards overfitting. Impurity importance gave 18.7% of its total to fifteen pure-noise columns; permutation importance gave them 0.22% of its own, much smaller total. Prefer permutation importance, and remember it can mark both halves of a correlated pair as unimportant, since each one covers for the other.

L1 selects during training and keeps one column from each correlated pair. At C = 0.03 it recovered all five informative columns plus one copy and no noise; at C = 0.3, eleven noise columns were back. Which member of a pair survives is arbitrary and can flip on the next resample.

Selecting on the whole dataset, then cross-validating, is leakage. On 2,000 pure-noise columns and a coin-flip target, it reported 74.5% accuracy; done properly, 47.5%. The inflation grows with the number of features searched, from 0.59 at 50 columns to 0.82 at 5,000.

The size of that leak tracks the instability of the selection. On the real dataset, all five folds chose the identical ten columns, and the leak was worth exactly nothing. On the noise problem, one column was common to all folds and 68 were chosen by at least one. Comparing per-fold selections tells you which regime you're in, though that check itself only works if you do the selection properly to begin with.

Different methods select different features, and none of them is simply the truth. Five methods produced three distinct answers here and agreed on only three columns; only L1 recovered all five informative ones. Treat agreement across methods as confirmation, disagreement as a genuine question worth chasing, and cross-validated score as the only real arbiter between them.
