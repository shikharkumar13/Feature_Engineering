Data Understanding and Basic Cleaning

Notebook 1 assumed clean data so it could focus on what a feature is. This one removes that assumption. Using a real, messy passenger record with genuine gaps and genuine extremes, it measures — rather than asserts — what a careless repair actually costs, down to fractions of a year and double-digit percentages of a column's variance.

Datasets: Titanic (891 rows) & Telco Churn (7,043 rows) Stack: pandas · scikit-learn · matplotlib Companion to: Notebook 2

Cleaning has a reputation as the boring part, the plumbing you get through on the way to the interesting modeling work. That reputation survives mostly because most treatments of it never show you a number. This article does not have that luxury, because the notebook it accompanies does not either: every claim below is a measurement, not an assertion, and several of them are uncomfortable. Filling a missing age with the column average sounds like the most harmless thing you can do to a dataset. Under one set of conditions it is. Under another, demonstrated with real numbers later in this notebook, the exact same technique shifts a column's mean by three and a half years in one experiment and, in a separate one, quietly deletes 18% of that column's variance, and pandas will not print a single warning while either happens.

Notebook 1 built the vocabulary for what a feature is and where feature engineering sits in a project. This notebook covers the stage immediately before it: understanding what you actually have, and repairing what is wrong with it, before you engineer anything on top. Eight ideas, in order: profiling a table properly, the three distinct reasons a value can be missing, the fact that absence itself can be a signal, scoring imputation methods against ground truth instead of just watching a NaN count hit zero, the same train/test leakage rule from notebook 1 applied to imputers specifically, telling a data error apart from a genuine extreme, duplicate records that hide behind a unique ID, and the quiet cost of a category typed four different ways by four different people.

1Profiling before you change anything

You do not clean first. You look. Every repair destroys the evidence of what was originally wrong

That's the whole discipline of this section in one sentence, and it's worth taking literally. The moment you fill a missing value, drop a row, or overwrite an entry, the raw state that produced your decision is gone. If the decision was wrong, there's no undo that recovers what the data looked like before you touched it, short of reloading the original file. The profiling pass is the only point in the entire project where you see the data exactly as it arrived.

describe() is probably the most over-trusted single command in pandas, and it earns that skepticism honestly. Two blind spots matter here directly, and a third is subtler.

It silently drops every non-numeric column. Five of Titanic's twelve columns, Name, Sex, Ticket, Cabin, and Embarked, are simply absent from that table. Nothing wrong with any of them would ever show up here, because the command never looked.

Its count row is the only hint of missing data, and it's easy to read past. Age shows 714 while every other numeric column shows 891. There's no color, no flag, no asterisk: just a smaller number sitting in a row most people skim on the way to the more visually interesting mean and std rows beneath it.

The third trap is quieter still. Look at the Fare row: the mean is 32.20 while the median is 14.45, and the max is 512.33. A mean more than double the median is the numeric signature of a long right tail, a detail describe() hands you without ever telling you what it means. Section 6 spends real time on exactly this column.

A dedicated missingness report is a better first move than leaning on describe() to surface gaps it wasn't built to highlight.

A missingness report, built on purpose
def missing_report(df):
    # One row per column that has at least one missing value
    missing = df.isna().sum()
    report = pd.DataFrame({
        'missing': missing,
        'pct': (missing / len(df) * 100).round(1),
        'dtype': df.dtypes.astype(str),
        'n_unique': df.nunique(),
    })
    return report[report['missing'] > 0].sort_values('missing', ascending=False)

2The three mechanisms behind missing data

Vocabulary that interviews ask about and tutorials skip, because it's usually taught as terms to memorize rather than something with consequences you can measure

The three mechanisms describe why a value is absent, not how much is absent or where. All three can produce the exact same percentage missing in the exact same column, and still demand completely different responses, because the question they answer is about cause, not quantity.

  • MCAR, Missing Completely At Random. The probability of a value being missing has nothing to do with anything, observed or unobserved. A lab technician drops a tray of samples. The rows that survive are a smaller, but fair, sample of the original population.
  • MAR, Missing At Random. The probability of being missing depends on data you can see. Older survey respondents skip the income question more often than younger ones, and you have everyone's age. Because the missingness is predictable from a column you already have, that column can be used to recover what was lost.
  • MNAR, Missing Not At Random. The probability of being missing depends on the missing value itself. High earners decline to state their income, and how much they earn is exactly the thing you don't have. No amount of clever modeling with your other columns can fully repair this, because the reason for the gap is the number that would have filled it.

The names are unhelpfully similar on the page, so it's worth collapsing all three definitions down to the one question that actually distinguishes them: what does the probability of being missing depend on? Nothing. Something you have. Or the value itself. Titanic gives us real, checkable evidence for the first two.

Knowing that a dependency exists is useful. It doesn't yet tell you how much damage ignoring it would do. For that, Titanic offers something most real datasets never will: a column, Age, where we happen to know every true value for the 714 passengers it's recorded for. That lets us run an experiment no live missing-data problem permits. Hide some of those known values under each of the three mechanisms in turn, then check the damage against ground truth.

Setting up three hiding rules from one random draw
# The 714 passengers whose age IS recorded become our known population.
# We hide values from it, so we can compare against the truth afterwards.
known = titanic[titanic['Age'].notna()].copy().reset_index(drop=True)
true_age = known['Age'].values
n = len(known)
true_mean = true_age.mean()

rng = np.random.default_rng(RANDOM_STATE)
u = rng.random(n)        # ONE random draw, reused for all three mechanisms

# Only the RULE for what gets hidden differs between these three.
p_mcar = np.full(n, 0.35)                                          # depends on nothing
p_mar = known['Pclass'].map({1: 0.10, 2: 0.25, 3: 0.55}).values    # depends on Pclass (observed)
p_mnar = np.where(true_age > np.median(true_age), 0.55, 0.12)      # depends on Age itself (hidden)

print(f'Known population : {n} passengers')
print(f'True mean age    : {true_mean:.3f} years')
Output
Known population : 714 passengers
True mean age    : 29.699 years

The design of that cell deserves a slow read, because the trick it uses is what makes the comparison fair. A single array of 714 random numbers, u, is drawn once and reused for all three mechanisms. What changes between MCAR, MAR, and MNAR is never the randomness itself, only the rule that decides which values it hides: a flat 35% for everyone under MCAR, a rate that depends on Pclass under MAR, and a rate that depends on Age itself under MNAR. Reusing the same draw means any difference in outcome between the three can only be attributed to the rule, never to which passengers randomly happened to get unlucky.

Hiding real ages three ways, then comparing to the truth we still have
mechanisms = {'MCAR': p_mcar, 'MAR': p_mar, 'MNAR': p_mnar}
masks = {}

print(f"{'mechanism':10s} {'% hidden':>9s} {'mean of what remains':>22s} {'bias':>9s}")
print('-' * 54)
for name, prob in mechanisms.items():
    hidden = u < prob                 # same u every time, different threshold
    masks[name] = hidden
    observed_mean = true_age[~hidden].mean()

Three mechanisms, one random draw, three completely different outcomes.

MCAR is the harmless case. A bias of +0.061 years on a true mean of 29.699. Thirty-four and a half percent of the column was deleted and the survivors still represent the original population almost exactly. This is the whole reason MCAR is the comfortable diagnosis to receive: dropping the affected rows costs you sample size and, to a first approximation, nothing else.

MAR biases the result, from the identical random draw. A bias of +1.592 years. Third class passengers were removed more aggressively, and third class passengers skewed younger, so the survivors' average drifted upward. Notice what wasn't used to decide who got hidden: nobody's age was ever consulted. The bias arrived entirely by way of Pclass, a column that, if used correctly, could also have been used to correct for it.

MNAR is the dangerous one. A bias of −3.526 years. Here the hiding rule looked directly at age, removing older passengers more often, and the surviving mean is wrong by more than three and a half years in a column whose overall standard deviation is 14.5. A quarter of a standard deviation of pure, silent bias, in a case built to be about as tame as MNAR gets.

What each mechanism licenses you to do about it, stated as plainly as the evidence supports:

  • Under MCAR, dropping the incomplete rows is safe, and simple imputation is unbiased.
  • Under MAR, dropping rows introduces bias, but the columns you already have contain what's needed to correct for it. This is precisely the situation multivariate imputers like KNN and Iterative are built to exploit, which section 4 measures directly.
  • Under MNAR, no imputation method can fully rescue you, because the information required was never collected in the first place. The honest responses are to model the missingness explicitly, go collect better data, or state the limitation plainly rather than paper over it.
The uncomfortable part

You usually cannot tell which mechanism you're facing from the data alone. Distinguishing MAR from MNAR requires knowing the very values you don't have, which is a circular requirement by construction. What you genuinely can do is test whether missingness is predictable from the columns you've observed, the way the crosstab above did for Cabin and Pclass. A real dependency there rules out MCAR with confidence. It does not rule in MAR over MNAR, because an observed correlation is consistent with both: the missingness could depend purely on Pclass (MAR), or it could depend on Pclass and on the hidden value itself (MNAR), and nothing in the observed data can tell those two apart. Anyone who tells you they statistically verified MCAR is describing a test that rules things out, not one that proves the remaining case correct.

3Missingness can itself be information

The reflex is to treat every gap as damage to repair. Sometimes the pattern of absence is the signal

Fill a column in and whatever the gap was telling you disappears into whichever value you chose to write over it. Before repairing anything, it's worth checking whether the absence itself was carrying a message.

That doesn't make the technique wrong. It makes this particular application marginal, and the distinction generalizes into a habit worth keeping: check whether a new feature is genuinely new, or a restatement of something already present. A feature that correlates strongly with an existing one can only ever contribute whatever is left over after the overlap is accounted for, and it's worth knowing that going in rather than being surprised by a small number later.

Missing-indicators earn their place clearly when absence is driven by something not otherwise present in your data: a blank income field on a loan application, a skipped medical question, a sensor that reports nothing specifically when it's faulty. In those cases the indicator is carrying information no other column has, rather than a faint echo of one that's already there.

Scikit-learn builds this in directly, which beats constructing the columns by hand for one concrete reason: a hand-built column has to be remembered and reapplied at every future prediction, while a parameter on a transformer survives automatically into a pipeline.

4Imputation strategies, scored against the truth

A dropping NaN count proves the code ran. It says nothing about whether the filled-in values are any good

Most introductions to imputation show a before-and-after NaN count and stop there. That proves the imputer executed without crashing. It says nothing about the only thing that actually matters afterward, which is whether the number that got written in is close to reality. We can do better here, because for Age we still have the ground truth sitting in true_age. Reusing the MAR mask from section 2, we hide 247 real ages, impute them five different ways, and check each guess against the value we know was really there.

Two numbers get tracked for every strategy:

  • RMSE against the true hidden ages. Lower means the filled-in guesses were closer to reality.
  • The standard deviation of the column afterward, compared against the true value of 14.52. An imputer that pushes many rows toward the same handful of values quietly shrinks this number, which weakens every correlation and every model that column later takes part in, with no error or warning attached to the damage.

Before reading the results, it's worth knowing what each of these five is actually doing, since the comparison means little without it.

Mean and median are univariate: each ignores every other column and fills every gap with the same single number, the average or the middle value of whatever rows in that column aren't missing. Fast, always available, and blind to anything the other five columns might know about a particular row.

KNN looks at the other columns. For a passenger missing Age, it finds the five most similar passengers by Euclidean distance across Pclass, SibSp, Parch, Fare, and is_male, then averages their ages. Distance is the whole mechanism, which is exactly why the code scales every column with StandardScaler before calling KNNImputer: without scaling, Fare, which ranges into the hundreds, would dominate the distance calculation and is_male, which is only ever 0 or 1, would contribute almost nothing to it. Distance-based methods need this in a way plain logistic regression, as used in notebook 1, does not: a raw Euclidean distance is not just slower to fit on unscaled columns, it's a fundamentally different, wrong answer, since whichever column happens to have the largest numbers silently dominates every comparison.

Iterative imputation (the scikit-learn implementation of an algorithm usually called MICE, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations) goes a step further: it treats Age as the target of its own small regression model, trained on the other columns, refills the gaps with that model's predictions, then cycles through and repeats for every other column with missing values, feeding each column's improved estimates back into the next round. Over several passes, the estimates stabilize. It's more expensive than KNN and often more accurate, because it's explicitly modeling the relationship rather than averaging nearby neighbors.

Group median is the low-tech entrant: the median age within each combination of Pclass and sex. No model, no distance calculation, just six numbers looked up from a table.

A practical summary of when to reach for what

  • Mean or median when a column has very little missing data, or you need something fast and defensible as a first baseline. Median for skewed columns, mean for roughly symmetric ones.
  • Mode, or a constant like 'Unknown', for categorical columns, where there's no such thing as the mean of "Southampton." A constant is often the better of the two, since it preserves the fact that the value was genuinely absent instead of quietly asserting a specific, possibly wrong, category.
  • A groupby median whenever you already know a sensible grouping the value depends on. Frequently the best return on effort of anything on this list.
  • KNN or Iterative when the mechanism looks like MAR, the columns are meaningfully correlated, and the accuracy gain is worth the computational cost. Both need numeric input on a common scale, and both get slower as the row count grows.
  • Leave it missing if the model you're using handles NaN natively. LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost all learn a default routing direction for missing values during training, which is frequently better than anything you would have imputed by hand.

Categorical columns need their own version of this same discipline, since there's no arithmetic mean of a port of embarkation.

5Imputation is a transformer, so the leakage rule applies

Filling gaps feels like cleaning. Cleaning feels like something you do to the whole file before splitting. Both feelings are the trap

Notebook 1 established the rule that governs every transformer you will ever fit: every statistic it learns has to come from training rows only. Imputation is the single easiest place to break that rule in practice, precisely because filling missing values doesn't feel like modeling the way a scaler or an encoder does. It feels like tidying up, and tidying up feels like something reasonable to do to the whole dataset before you've even thought about splitting it.

The median used to fill a gap is a learned statistic in exactly the same sense as a scaler's mean or standard deviation. It's computed from data, and if that data includes rows that are supposed to be held out for evaluation, the same leak from notebook 1 has happened again, just wearing a cleaning costume instead of a modeling one.

The pipeline stores 28.50, the training median, and applies precisely that number to the test rows when it transforms them. It never looks at the test data to decide what to fill it with, in the same way the scaler in notebook 1 never recomputed its mean on test rows.

There's a second reason this matters that has nothing to do with the honesty of an evaluation score. When this model eventually runs in production, scoring one new passenger, one new loan applicant, one new customer at a time, there is no column to take a median of. A single row has no distribution of its own. The imputer has to arrive already carrying a number it learned earlier, during training, which is exactly what fitting on training data produces and exactly what code like df['Age'].fillna(df['Age'].median()) cannot supply. That line only works when df is the whole file sitting in front of you. It has nothing to say about a single record arriving alone, which is the situation almost every deployed model actually faces.

6Outliers, and the question most tutorials skip

Detecting an outlier is mechanical. Deciding whether it's an error or the truth is the part that actually decides your result

An outlier is a value far from the rest of the data. Finding candidates is a solved, mechanical problem that every tutorial covers. The question that comes after detection, and the one that actually determines what you should do, is much harder and gets skipped far more often: is this value wrong, or is it real?

Those two situations demand opposite treatments. A typo that entered a salary as 2,000,000 instead of 200,000 should be corrected or removed; leaving it in place lets one keystroke error dominate an entire model. A genuine billionaire sitting in an income dataset is not an error, and deleting them means your model no longer describes the population you claimed to be studying. Titanic's Fare column hands us a real, checkable example of each case.

The z-score result is the interesting failure, and it's worth understanding exactly why it under-performs rather than just noting that it does. It flags only 20 passengers, far fewer than the IQR method's 116, because of a real and well-documented trap called masking: the z-score is computed from the mean and standard deviation of the very column it's supposed to be screening, and both of those statistics are themselves dragged around by the extreme values they're meant to catch. The more extreme the outliers, the more they inflate the standard deviation, which widens the threshold a value needs to cross to count as extreme, which then lets the very same outliers slip under the newly raised bar.

This is precisely why quartiles, the median, and the modified z-score (which substitutes the median and the median absolute deviation, MAD, for the mean and standard deviation) are the preferred tools on skewed data: they are robust statistics, meaning a handful of extreme values can barely move them. The plain mean and standard deviation are not robust, and get pulled toward the exact values they're supposed to be measuring distance from.

A working rule for choosing a method

Use IQR or the modified z-score by default, especially on skewed data. Use the plain z-score only once you've confirmed the column is roughly symmetric. And treat a large disagreement between methods as a signal in its own right: it usually means the distribution is skewed enough that any mean-based method shouldn't be trusted on it at all.

Barely any difference, and the correction wins only 2 folds out of 5, which is the signature of noise rather than genuine improvement. The repair is still the right thing to have done, and it's worth being clear about why that's true even though the metric shrugged: fixing a real misunderstanding of what a column measures is correct whether or not a particular downstream score happens to notice. Here, survival was driven overwhelmingly by class and sex, and Fare was already substantially restating class, so the correction had little room left to help this specific question. Asked a different question, such as predicting what an individual actually paid for their own passage, the same fix would matter enormously. A correction that changes nothing about today's model can still be exactly what stops you from drawing a wrong conclusion on tomorrow's.

Treating outliers you've decided are genuinely real

When a value is truly extreme rather than wrong, four options exist, and they are not interchangeable.

The log transform is the clear winner by this measure, taking skew from 4.79 down to 0.39 while keeping every single row and preserving the relative ordering of every passenger's fare. Capping only reaches 3.11, because even the stretch of values below the 99th percentile is still a fairly long tail in its own right.

  • Capping, also called winsorising, keeps every row and limits how much influence the extremes can exert, at the cost of inventing values: three passengers who genuinely paid different amounts can end up recorded as having paid identically. Reach for it when the extremes are plausible but you don't want a handful of them dominating a model's fit.
  • A log or square root transform compresses the tail without deleting or fabricating anything, and is usually the right first choice for positively skewed data like money, counts, and durations. It can't handle negative values, and log1p specifically handles zeros safely, which matters directly for a column that includes fifteen genuine zeros. Notebook 3 covers this transform properly.
  • Removal is the bluntest tool, and here it would discard 13.0% of all passengers. Reserve it for values you've positively identified as errors, not merely as extreme.
  • Keeping the value and adding a flag column lets the model itself decide how much weight to give an extreme case. Often the best choice when you're genuinely unsure whether an extreme value is real.

The one move to avoid entirely is applying any of these four automatically across every numeric column because it makes the notebook tidier. Each choice encodes a specific claim about what the underlying data means, and that claim has to be made on purpose, column by column.

7Duplicate records

They inflate your data's apparent size and let the same information sit in both your training and test sets, for the same reason leakage does

drop_duplicates() handles the easy case, an entire row repeated byte for byte, without any trouble. The harder case is a record duplicated in substance but not in its exact bytes, and that's precisely where the supposedly spotless Telco dataset turns out to have been hiding something all along.

This is the single most common way duplicate detection quietly fails in practice. An auto-incrementing ID, an ingestion timestamp, or a source filename guarantees that no two rows will ever be byte-identical, and drop_duplicates() will then confidently report a clean bill of health on data that is, in substance, full of repeats.

When you do decide to deduplicate, the keep argument decides which copy of a group survives, and that choice matters enormously whenever the rows in a group aren't truly identical, such as when they represent the same entity at two different points in time.

8Inconsistent category labels

Whenever a value is typed by a human instead of chosen from a dropdown, the same real-world category arrives spelled several different ways

New York, NY, new york, and New  York with a stray double space are four entirely distinct strings as far as pandas is concerned, and exactly one city as far as everyone else is concerned. The damage this causes is quiet rather than dramatic: each spelling becomes its own separate category, one-hot encoding produces four sparse columns where one solid one belongs, and each of those four ends up with too few rows for a model to learn anything reliable from it.

Both Titanic and Telco happen to arrive already normalized, so the example below is constructed on purpose. The technique is still worth practicing deliberately, because raw operational data collected from real forms, call centers, or spreadsheets edited by several people almost always needs exactly this treatment.

Mapping abbreviations requires real knowledge, and carries real risk. Only someone who already knows the domain can say with confidence that NY means New York rather than, say, a typo for something else entirely. This step deserves review specifically because it can fail silently: a wrong mapping doesn't throw an error, it just quietly merges two genuinely different categories into one, which is a worse outcome than having left them apart as separate, if messy, categories.

A few practical habits worth carrying into real, messier data than this constructed example:

  • Print the value counts before and after every cleaning step. It's the fastest way to notice a mapping that merged more than it should have.
  • Look specifically at the long tail. Categories appearing once or twice in a large column are exactly where misspellings and one-off data entry mistakes hide.
  • For a large, genuinely messy column, a fuzzy-matching library such as rapidfuzz can propose candidate groupings by string similarity. Treat its output as suggestions for a person to approve, never as a finished answer on its own, because fuzzy matching will just as cheerfully merge Springfield, IL with Springfield, MA as it will fix a genuine typo.
  • Fix the source when that's within reach. A dropdown menu on the data entry form prevents more of this problem than any amount of downstream cleaning ever will.

The same reasoning extends past city names to inconsistent units (pounds sitting in the same column as kilograms), inconsistent date formats, and inconsistent codes for missing data, where a single column might use -1, 999, the string 'N/A', and a genuinely empty string to all mean the same absence. Notebook 1 already met one version of this problem, where a blank string in TotalCharges was counted as present by .info() simply because it wasn't technically null.

Key things to remember from this notebook

Profile before you touch anything, and don't lean on describe() to do it. It silently drops every non-numeric column and reports missing data only through a count row that's easy to skim past. Write a dedicated missingness report and plot where the gaps fall, since a pattern in the map tells you things a bare percentage cannot.

The mechanism behind a gap decides what you're allowed to do about it. Hiding 35% of a column three different ways, from one identical random draw, biased the mean by +0.061 years under MCAR, +1.592 under MAR, and −3.526 under MNAR. Under MCAR, dropping rows is safe. Under MAR, the columns you already have can repair the damage, which is what KNN and Iterative imputation exist for. Under MNAR, no method fully recovers what's missing, and saying so plainly beats imputing and hoping.

Absence can be information in its own right. Passengers with a recorded cabin survived at 66.7% against 30.0% for those without one. The gain from adding that as a feature here was small and won only 4 folds of 5, because cabin_missing largely restates Pclass, which is exactly the redundancy check worth running before trusting any new feature.

Score imputers against ground truth, not against a shrinking NaN count. Iterative imputation won at RMSE 11.999 and mean imputation lost at 14.514, but a plain median within Pclass and sex groups reached 12.926 using four lines of pandas and no model fitting at all. Mean imputation also destroyed 18.4% of the column's spread by stacking 247 passengers onto a single value, a cost no error message will ever surface.

Imputers are transformers, so fit them on training data only. Titanic's age median is 28.00 on the full file and 28.50 on the training split alone. Past the honesty of an evaluation score, a model serving one row at a time has no column to take a median of, so that number has to have already been learned before it arrives.

Outlier detection is not an objective, single-answer procedure. The same Fare column yielded 116 flagged rows under IQR, 20 under a plain z-score, and 171 under a modified z-score. The z-score under-reported specifically because three extreme fares inflated the standard deviation that defines its own threshold: the cutoff sits at 181.28 with those fares included, and would drop to 154.11 without them, so their presence is what let them slip under their own bar.

The question after detection matters more than detection itself. Titanic's highest fares weren't errors; they were one ticket shared across three people, and dividing by party size cut the outlier count roughly in half without deleting a single row. The zero fares turned out to be crew and staff who genuinely paid nothing. Most apparent outliers are definition problems rather than data problems, so check what a column actually means before capping or deleting anything in it.

Duplicates hide behind unique identifiers. Telco reported zero duplicate rows and had 22 the moment customerID was set aside. Deduplicate on the columns that describe the world, never on the ones a system merely assigned, and always sort explicitly before choosing keep='first' or keep='last'.
